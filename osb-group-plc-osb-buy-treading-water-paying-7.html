Treading Water, Paying 7%

Nine-month trading update to 30 September 2026, with profit figures from the interim results to 30 June 2026. All figures in sterling; per-share figures in pence unless stated.

Briefing

  • Thesis: A profitable, well capitalised bank that is struggling to grow. Deposits are up 3% in nine months while the net loan book is up only 1.9%, and the margin is still compressing — but the shares are priced as though something is badly wrong, and it isn't.
  • The numbers: I expect a flat £286m post-tax for the full year, which after additional tier 1 coupons is 81.5p a share on today's count. 81.5p with 2% growth and an 8× multiple gives 756.32p against 507.75p — a +49.0% value opportunity.
  • Key strength: £550m of buybacks since 2022, a quarter of the share count retired, and a 7.0% dividend yield. The buybacks have absorbed close to half of the fall in profit at the per-share level.
  • Key risk: Margin compression has not finished — 2026 is guided at 215 to 220 basis points against 223 in the first half — and Basel 3.1 takes around 1.2 percentage points off the capital ratio in January.
  • Overall stance: Buy. A lacklustre situation rather than a bad one. I'm not expecting earnings to move far forwards or backwards over the next twelve months, and you're paid 7% to wait.

Business Model

OSB Group is a bank in the plainest sense. It takes in retail savings at one rate and lends them out as mortgages at a higher one, and the difference — the net interest margin — is nearly all of its income.

The lending side is built around professional landlords. Buy-to-let was £17.8bn of the £26.4bn gross loan book at the end of June, 68% of it, with residential mortgages at 19%, commercial mortgages 7% and development finance, asset finance and bridging around 2% each. The company's aim is to bring buy-to-let down to 60% or less of the book by 2029 by growing the higher-yielding specialist lines faster.

All of the mortgages come through brokers and independent financial advisers rather than branches.

The funding side is £25.0bn of retail deposits from Kent Reliance and Charter Savings Bank, which covers almost the whole loan book, topped up with securitisations, senior notes and central bank facilities.

Costs are low. Administrative expenses run at around £270m to £280m a year against net interest income of around £680m — a cost to income ratio of 40%, helped by a back office in India.

The brands

  • Rely: the new buy-to-let brand launched in November 2025 on the group's new lending platform, which now takes all of the new buy-to-let lending.
  • Precise: residential and bridging mortgages.
  • InterBay Commercial: commercial and semi-commercial mortgages and the more complex buy-to-let cases.
  • Kent Reliance and Charter Savings Bank: the two retail savings brands, with Kent Reliance's own mortgage lending in run-off since late 2025.
  • Development finance and asset finance: the smaller, higher-yielding parts of the book.

The business is reported as two segments, which are the two banks that came together in October 2019:

  • OneSavings Bank, which holds Kent Reliance, InterBay Commercial, Rely and the development and asset finance lines, with a net loan book of £17.2bn at the end of June.
  • Charter Court Financial Services, which holds Precise and Charter Savings Bank, with a net loan book of £9.0bn.

The group began trading as a bank in February 2011, listed in June 2014 and joined the FTSE 250 in June 2015.

Most of the mortgages are fixed for two or five years and the deposits are a mix of fixed-term bonds and easy access accounts, with the mismatch hedged with swaps — so the margin depends on what the company pays over SONIA (the Sterling Overnight Index Average, the benchmark rate banks price short-term sterling funding against) for new savings, against what it earns over swap rates on new mortgages.

2022 was the high point, a net interest margin of 278 basis points, because savings rates lagged the rise in base rate that year. Competition for retail savings has since taken that back to 221 basis points in 2024, 228 in 2025 and 223 in the first half of this year, and the guidance for 2026 is 215 to 220.

🎓 Clarity What is a "net interest margin", and what is a basis point? The net interest margin, or NIM, is the gap between what a bank earns on its loans and what it pays on its savings, expressed as a percentage of the money it has lent out. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 223 basis points is 2.23%. It sounds tiny, but on a £26bn loan book every single basis point is roughly £2.6m of income a year — which is why the fall from 278 basis points in 2022 to a guided 215 to 220 this year is the single biggest thing that has happened to this company's profits.

The effective interest rate adjustment

When a fixed-rate mortgage ends, the borrower moves on to a higher reversion rate until they refinance, and the company spreads the interest it expects to earn over the whole expected life of the loan, including the months it expects the customer to sit on the reversion rate. That spreading is the effective interest rate method — income is recognised early, on an assumption about how long customers stay.

In 2023 Precise customers refinanced far faster than assumed, with the expected time on reversion falling to around five months from around seventeen, and the company took a -£210.7m adjustment to net interest income. That is the main reason 2023's profit fell to £374.3m from £531.5m. The sensitivity is still there: a two-month change in the weighted average life is worth around £16m.

The Nine Months to 30 September 2026

The net loan book is up 1.9% since the end of December to £26,423.2m, which is a little disappointing. Originations were £3.5bn, up 4% on the same nine months of 2025, with buy-to-let and residential strong and the higher-yielding lines a little lower because of the uncertain economy and higher mortgage rates.

Total assets reduced by -0.5% to £30,959.2m, which the company puts down to trimming the liquidity portfolio.

Retail deposits did rather better, rising 3.0% to £24,986.8m, an extra £735.7m. Nearly all of that came in the first half; between June and September the deposit book was flat and the group drew £400m of central bank facilities instead, taking the balance outstanding to £656.8m from £1.5bn at the start of the year.

The common equity tier 1 ratio slipped 40 basis points to 15.4%, after the £100m share buyback announced in March was completed. That is still okay in truth, but I don't like that it has gone down.

Three months plus arrears were down 10 basis points to 1.6%, which is good, and were stable through the third quarter.

The First Half: Profit and Margin

The last set of profit figures we have are the interims for the six months to 30 June 2026. Profit before tax (PBT — what the company earned before the tax bill) was £187.2m, down -3% from £192.3m, while basic earnings per share (EPS — profit attributable to ordinary shareholders divided by the number of shares) rose 3% to 38.5p, because there were fewer shares in issue.

Net interest income edged up 1% to £339.8m, but the net interest margin fell to 223 basis points from 230, as the new retail savings the group has been raising cost more over SONIA than the money it replaced.

The impairment charge — the money set aside against loans expected to go bad — went up to £15.8m from £2.0m, a loan loss ratio of 12 basis points against 2, which the company says is in line with its long-term average. Return on tangible equity (ROTE — profit measured against the hard shareholder capital in the business) was 13.3% against 13.7%, and the interim dividend went up 5% to 11.8p.

What Matters for This Company

Deposits are growing, loans are not

We're seeing retail deposits increase by 3% while loan volumes are struggling. The net loan book is up £502.6m in nine months, and only £165.7m of that came in the third quarter.

The 2026 guidance is for net loan book growth broadly similar to 2025's 3.2%. To get there the book needs to reach around £26.75bn by December, another £330m or so in the final quarter against the £166m added in the third. The chief executive says growth to the end of October was in line with the company's expectations.

The company says it is writing new business at sustainable margins, and the blended cost of new retail funds in the quarter was slightly better than the revised assumption of SONIA plus 40 basis points it made at the interims. The guidance at the interims assumed no easing in retail funding costs for the rest of the year.

Alongside the update the company launched a tender offer for its £300m 9.5% notes due September 2028 and £400m 8.875% notes due January 2030. These were issued to meet the minimum requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities (MREL — the stack of debt a bank must hold that can be written down if it fails), which from 1 January 2026 equals the minimum capital requirement, so they are no longer needed. The company expects a positive financial benefit from the tender through 2029.

The buy-to-let book and the new platform

The company is progressing with buy-to-let mortgages on its new platform and has now reached £1bn of balance sheet from it.

The platform launched to the whole market in November 2025 under the new Rely brand after a pilot with more than 50 broker firms. All new buy-to-let lending has gone through it since, with the Precise and Kent Reliance buy-to-let ranges withdrawn and in run-off. The platform has 18 partner integrations, gives an agreement in principle in under ten minutes and uses automated valuations on around 10% of cases, where the old process used none.

In the first half of 2026 the group wrote £1,028.0m of buy-to-let, up 10% on £935.4m, nearly all of it through Rely, and by the end of September the balances on the platform had passed £1bn. Residential lending joined the platform in the third quarter under the Precise brand, with 65% of applications reaching offer in under five days.

The platform is a year old, so the three-year history is the group's buy-to-let lending across the brands that came before it.

Since then the originations have been rebuilt through the OneSavings Bank brands, from £1,372.3m in 2024 to £1,754.7m in 2025 and £1,006.4m in the first half of this year, while Precise buy-to-let has run down to almost nothing.

The gross buy-to-let book has been flat at £17.6bn to £17.8bn for two years, which is the plan: it is meant to fall as a share of the book, not in size.

🎓 Clarity What is a securitisation, and why does it shrink the loan book? A securitisation is where a lender bundles a pile of mortgages together and sells them to investors as bonds, taking cash up front instead of the interest over time. When OSB securitised £1.25bn of Precise buy-to-let loans in December 2024, those loans came off its balance sheet — so the book fell without a single borrower leaving or defaulting. It matters here because it explains why the buy-to-let book appears to shrink in 2024: that was a deliberate funding decision, not weak lending.

Buybacks

The company has completed the £100m share repurchase programme that it announced in March. 19.4m shares were bought at an average of 515p and the programme finished on 19 October.

Since 2022 the company has repurchased £550m of shares, representing 34% of its market capitalisation at 3 November 2026. That is a lot and quite impressive, and boosts the earnings per share figures quite considerably.

🎓 Clarity Why does a buyback make earnings per share rise when profit is falling? Earnings per share is total profit divided by the number of shares. When a company buys its own shares and cancels them, the same profit is shared between fewer owners, so each remaining share is entitled to more. At OSB, profit after tax fell -30% between 2022 and 2025 but earnings per share fell only -17%, because a quarter of the shares had gone. It matters for the valuation below, because the per-share figure I use rests on today's reduced share count, not the one from four years ago.

Capital

The common equity tier 1 ratio of 15.4% is after the buyback, and after risk-weighted assets grew 1.6% against the 1.9% growth in the loan book.

Basel 3.1 comes in on 1 January 2027 and the company expects it to take around 1.2 percentage points off the ratio through a 9% uplift in risk-weighted assets, which on the June position left a pro forma ratio of around 14%. Against that, the Pillar 2A requirement — the extra capital the regulator demands on top of the standard minimum for risks specific to that bank — is expected to fall by 55 basis points, taking the minimum capital requirement to 8.9% from 9.5%.

The board's target is a ratio of 13% to 13.5% once Basel 3.1 is in. Each percentage point of the ratio is around £127m on today's risk-weighted assets. The company says it will review its capital needs, its capital target and shareholder distributions for 2027 once the requirements are clearer, and plans an investor update in the first half of 2027.

Andy Golding stepped down as chief executive on 31 August 2026 and Enrique Alvarez Labiano took over on 1 September. Fiona Clutterbuck joins the boards as chair designate on 9 November 2026.

🎓 Clarity What are the CET1 ratio and risk-weighted assets? Common equity tier 1 (CET1) is the purest form of a bank's capital — ordinary shares and retained profits, the money that absorbs losses first. Risk-weighted assets (RWAs) are the bank's loans scaled by how risky the regulator judges them to be, so a mortgage counts for less than an unsecured loan. The CET1 ratio is one divided by the other: 15.4% here. It matters directly to shareholders because surplus capital above the board's 13% to 13.5% target is what funds the buybacks and special returns — and Basel 3.1, by lifting RWAs around 9%, consumes roughly 1.2 points of that surplus in January without a penny of real loss.

Pulling it together

OSB Group is a bank that is profitable, well capitalised but struggling to grow.

  • Retail deposits are up 3% in nine months and the loan book is up 1.9%, with the company choosing margin over volume in a market where the high street lenders are competing hard for buy-to-let and for savings.
  • Profit after tax has gone from £410m in 2022 to £286m in 2025, and the margin compression behind that has not finished, with 2026 guided at 215 to 220 basis points against 223 in the first half.
  • The buybacks have given shareholders a major reprieve. £550m since 2022 is 34% of the market capitalisation, a quarter of the shares have gone, and earnings per share has fallen by little more than half as much as profit.
  • The capital ratio of 15.4% still indicates a certain level of safety, and arrears coming down to 1.6% is good, but I don't like seeing the ratio slip, and Basel 3.1 takes another 1.2 points off it in January. The scale of buybacks from 2027 onwards depends on where the board lands between the 14% pro forma ratio and its 13% to 13.5% target.

My feelings are that this is a lacklustre situation rather than a bad one. The company is making profits and paying a 7.0% yield, the volumes are not moving positively, and given the macroeconomic environment in which they're working that may not be unexpected. I think they are likely to see items pick up, and the margin should be helped by the retirement of the expensive senior notes, but I'm not expecting the earnings to move far forwards or backwards over the next twelve months.

Valuation

Ultimately I'm expecting the company to achieve a very similar level of profitability to the previous year, basically flat overall at £286m post-tax profit for the full year. Taking off around £12.6m of coupon payments on the additional tier 1 notes, which is how the company itself arrives at basic earnings per share, leaves £273.4m for ordinary shareholders, and over the 335.357m shares now in issue that equates to 81.5p per share. That is the figure for today's share count; the basic earnings per share the company reports for 2026 will be lower, around 79p, because the shares bought back during the year were in issue for part of it.

£286m needs £144.7m in the second half against £141.3m in the first, 2.4% more, with the margin guided below the first half's 223 basis points and the impairment charge having risen — so it is achievable but not a given.

I'm using a growth rate of only 2%. That might be a little on the low side in truth, but this is a company that isn't seeing its volumes move positively. They are liquid, the capital ratio indicates safety within their borrowing, and they are managing to make some profitability, but they are struggling to grow. The medium-term target is mid single digit loan growth, and the net interest margin target of 215 to 220 basis points I think will be strengthened by likely further interest rate rises.

The multiple is 8×, with no valuation uplift or reduction applied, and the risk factor is set at 20%.

81.5p and 2% growth gives a natural valuation of 756.32p per share. Against the current share price of 507.75p that is a +49.0% value opportunity, so I'm giving this a buy.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong

  • Margin compression has not finished: the net interest margin fell to 223 basis points in the first half from 230, and the full-year guidance is 215 to 220. The guidance at the interims assumed no easing in retail funding costs for the rest of the year.
  • The second-half profit is not a given: £286m for the year needs £144.7m in the second half against £141.3m in the first, 2.4% more, with the margin guided below the first half's level and the impairment charge having risen. The 81.5p rests on that £286m.
  • Volumes are weak: the net loan book is up only £502.6m in nine months, and just £165.7m of that came in the third quarter. Hitting the guided growth needs around £330m in the final quarter.
  • Capital is going the wrong way: the CET1 ratio slipped 40 basis points to 15.4%, and Basel 3.1 takes around 1.2 percentage points off it on 1 January 2027, leaving a pro forma figure of around 14% on the June position.
  • The 2027 distribution decision is open: the scale of buybacks from 2027 depends on where the board lands between that 14% pro forma ratio and its 13% to 13.5% target. The capital review and investor update do not come until the first half of 2027.
  • Credit is normalising: the impairment charge rose to £15.8m from £2.0m, a loan loss ratio of 12 basis points against 2 — in line with the company's long-term average, but no longer a tailwind.
  • The effective interest rate sensitivity is still there: 2023 carried a -£210.7m adverse adjustment when Precise customers refinanced far faster than assumed, and a two-month change in the weighted average life is still worth around £16m.
  • Return on tangible equity has been cut: from low teens in March to around 12.5% at the interims, with the company flagging a possible modest impact on the 2027 mid-teens aspiration if funding cost pressures continue.
  • New people at the top: Enrique Alvarez Labiano took over as chief executive on 1 September 2026 and Fiona Clutterbuck joined as chair designate on 9 November 2026.

What Would Change My Mind

  • The loan book missing December: the book needs to reach around £26.75bn to hit the guided growth. The chief executive says growth to the end of October was in line with expectations — if the fourth quarter instead looks like the third's £166m, the growth case weakens.
  • The margin breaking below guidance: 215 to 220 basis points is the band. Blended new retail funding costs were slightly better than the revised SONIA plus 40 basis points assumption in the third quarter; if that reverses, the 2026 earnings expectation does too.
  • Earnings moving materially away from £286m post-tax: the whole valuation is the 81.5p. I'm not expecting earnings to move far forwards or backwards over the next twelve months, so a move either way is the thing that changes the arithmetic.
  • Capital landing worse than the pro forma: if Basel 3.1 costs more than the expected 1.2 percentage points, or the 2027 review sets a higher target than 13% to 13.5%, the buybacks that have been absorbing the profit decline get smaller.
  • Arrears turning: three months plus arrears came down 10 basis points to 1.6% and were stable through the third quarter. A rise from here, or a loan loss ratio moving above the long-term average, would change the picture.
  • A repeat of 2023: another adverse effective interest rate adjustment on faster-than-assumed refinancing.
  • On the upside: a positive financial benefit from the tender for the 9.5% and 8.875% notes through 2029, the back-book roll-off, and delivery of the mid-teens return on tangible equity in 2027 and 2028 would each support the margin recovering rather than simply stabilising.

Bottom Line — Buy

A lacklustre situation rather than a bad one. OSB is profitable, well capitalised and paying a 7.0% yield, but it is not growing: deposits up 3% in nine months, the loan book up 1.9%, and a margin that has gone from 278 basis points in 2022 to a guided 215 to 220 this year.

The offset is the buybacks. £550m since 2022, a quarter of the share count retired, and earnings per share falling -17% where profit after tax fell -30%. That is what makes the per-share arithmetic work.

I'm expecting a flat £286m post-tax for the year, 81.5p a share on today's count. At 2% growth and an 8× multiple that is 756.32p against a 507.75p share price. The risk factor is 20% and the probability 50% — this is a company I expect to stand still, not one I expect to re-rate on momentum.

Buy. Value opportunity: +49.0%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: not assigned.
