John Templeton: the global investor

John Templeton started out in a small town in Tennessee during the Depression and ended up running one of the first global mutual funds from a house in the Bahamas. Along the way he taught a generation of investors to look for bargains wherever in the world they happened to be.

  • 1912–2008
  • Templeton Growth Fund
  • Global bargains and contrarian investing

John Templeton was born in Winchester, Tennessee, on 29 November 1912, the second son of Harvey Templeton, a small-town lawyer who at one point owned around six farms, and Vella Handly Templeton, an elder of the local Presbyterian church who had studied Latin, Greek and mathematics. His parents placed few restrictions on him, so that as a boy he sold vegetables from his mother's garden and resold fireworks at a profit, and when his high school turned out not to teach the calculus he needed in order to apply to Yale, he persuaded the principal to let him set up and teach the course himself.

He arrived at Yale in 1930, but the Depression soon caught up with the family, and at the start of his second year his father told him that he could no longer contribute anything towards his education. John Templeton paid his way through the rest of the course with scholarships, part-time jobs and winnings from small-stakes poker, a game at which he became skilled and which, by his own account, he never played again. "At first this seemed like a tragedy, but now, looking back, it was the best thing that could have happened," he said many years later. He graduated in 1934 near the top of his class and as president of the Yale chapter of Phi Beta Kappa, and went on as a Rhodes Scholar to Balliol College, Oxford, where he took a law degree in 1936.

It was what he did next that set the direction of his career, because instead of going straight to work he spent the months after Oxford travelling around the world on a small budget, and the John Templeton Foundation traces his interest in opportunities outside the United States to this journey. He returned to America in May 1937, joined Fenner & Beane, a small investment firm in New York, and married Judith Folk the same year. The young couple resolved to save fifty cents of every dollar they earned and furnished a five-room flat for $25 by bidding at auctions.

From 1937 to 1940 John Templeton was vice-president of the National Geophysical Company, an oil exploration business in Dallas, and it was there that he made his best-known investment. In September 1939, as war began in Europe and American share prices stood at depressed levels, he borrowed $10,000 from his former boss at Fenner & Beane and instructed his broker to purchase $100 worth of every stock on the New York Stock Exchange trading at $1 a share or less. That came to 104 companies, 34 of which were already in bankruptcy, and his reasoning was that wartime demand would revive even weak businesses, whereas the market was pricing many of them as though they would disappear.

The outcome was that just four of the 104 turned out to be worthless, and when he sold the portfolio around four years later the $10,000 had grown to roughly $40,000. However, the story is often told without its risks, because he had purchased on borrowed money, a third of the companies were bankrupt when he purchased them, and the Dow was scarcely higher in September 1943 than in September 1939, so that the gain came from the recovery of individual distressed shares rather than from a rising market, and had his lender wanted the money back in 1940 or 1941 the result could have been very different.

In 1940 John Templeton sold his stake in National Geophysical and used the proceeds to purchase a small investment counselling firm in New York, which became Templeton, Dobbrow & Vance and, over time, the Templeton family of funds. The years that followed were hard ones at home, because his wife Judith died in 1951 and his mother within six months of her, leaving him with three young children, and he married again in 1958, to Irene Reynolds Butler.

Templeton Growth Fund, the vehicle on which his reputation rests, was launched on 29 November 1954, his 42nd birthday, with around $7m, and was incorporated in Canada rather than the United States. Its mandate was to invest anywhere in the world, which made it one of the first mutual funds through which North American savers could own shares in foreign companies.

The method behind the fund was set down most plainly in a list of maxims that William Proctor published in "The Templeton Touch", an authorised biography, in 1983. The best-known of them is the fourth: "The time of maximum pessimism is the best time to buy, and the time of maximum optimism is the best time to sell." The others explain how he put that into practice, because he argued that "too many investors focus on 'outlook' and 'trend'", so that "more profit is made by focusing on value", and that "if you search worldwide, you will find more bargains and better bargains than by studying only one nation". Putting this another way, the global mandate was not a view about which countries would grow fastest, but a way of widening the list of companies that might be cheap.

He added to these in 1993, in an article for World Monitor that Franklin Templeton still reprints as his sixteen rules for investment success. The first rule was to invest for "maximum total real return", meaning "the return on invested dollars after taxes and after inflation", and under the heading of learning from mistakes Mr Templeton wrote that the investor who says "'This time is different,' when in fact it's virtually a repeat of an earlier situation, has uttered among the four most costly words in the annals of investing." The line is usually quoted as "the four most dangerous words", a version newspapers were attributing to him by March 1993, although the earliest printed form of the saying is not his.

Japan was the clearest example of the method at work, because Templeton Growth Fund began purchasing Japanese shares in the 1960s, when few American fund managers paid them any attention, and by around 1970, as Mr Templeton told the Christian Science Monitor two decades later, half of his assets were in Japan "because that's where the bargains were". However, he was just as willing to leave, so that by the end of 1986 the Templeton World Fund had about 1% of its assets in Japan against roughly 50% a few years earlier, and Martin Flanagan, who later ran Franklin Templeton, recalled that in the late 1980s he refused to go back into a market trading at more than 50 times earnings. The Japanese market peaked at the end of 1989 and then fell for more than a decade, which meant that leaving early cost him far less than staying would have done.

In the late 1960s John Templeton left New York for Lyford Cay, near Nassau in the Bahamas, and gave up his American citizenship to become a naturalised British citizen. Accounts differ on the exact year of the renunciation, and the move has often been described as a tax decision, which he denied, saying that his clients' results were "far better here than when I had my office in 30 Rockefeller Plaza" because distance from Wall Street made it easier to think differently from everyone else. By 1981 he was managing $1.6bn in mutual funds for around 132,000 shareholders, with Templeton Growth Fund itself at $600m, and by 1990 the organisation ran about $18bn from offices in eight countries.

He was open about the limits of the method, telling the Christian Science Monitor in 1981 that he made a mistake on about one in three of his choices, that the fund held a share for around five years on average, and that his philosophy was "simple minded", which is to say that it depended on patience and a willingness to be unpopular rather than on any special insight into economies.

The record that resulted is usually summarised in a single illustration published by the John Templeton Foundation: $10,000 placed in the Class A shares of Templeton Growth Fund at launch in 1954, with dividends reinvested, would have grown to around $2m by 1992, when he sold the business. Over 38 years that works out at a compound rate of roughly 15% a year, and Franklin Templeton's own account at the time of his death put the figure at around 14.5%. The illustration does not say whether the initial sales charge, which was 8.5% on Templeton funds in the 1980s, has been deducted, and it does not show the path between the two endpoints or how the fund fared against a world index in any single year. It is a record of the past and not a forecast, and it is John Templeton's record only up to 1992, since the fund has now been run by other managers for longer than he ran it himself.

From the early 1970s onwards a growing share of his attention went elsewhere, because in 1972 he established the Templeton Prize for progress in religion, first awarded in 1973 to Mother Teresa, and insisted that its cash value should always exceed that of the Nobel prizes, so that it began at $85,000 and is now £1.1m. He published "The Humble Approach" in 1981 and set up the John Templeton Foundation in 1987 under the motto "How little we know, how eager to learn", and in the same year Queen Elizabeth II made him a Knight Bachelor for his philanthropy.

The business was sold on 31 July 1992, when Franklin Resources of California agreed to purchase Templeton, Galbraith & Hansberger, which by then managed $21.3bn across 78 funds, for $913m. John Templeton's own shares in the firm were valued at around $440m, a figure that some obituaries later mistook for the price of the whole business, and much of the proceeds went to his foundations. He was 79 and retired from managing money, although the funds kept his name, so that anything Templeton Growth Fund has done since belongs to his successors, and Franklin Templeton's September 2026 factsheet, which gives the Class A shares a compound return of 11.25% a year since 1954 before sales charges, blends his 38 years with 34 years of other managers.

He had not, however, finished investing on his own account. In early 2000, at the age of 87, he sold short a basket of around 84 Nasdaq technology companies whose shares had trebled from their flotation price, putting about $2.2m into each position, or roughly $185m in all, and timing each sale for the days before the lock-up period expired and insiders were first allowed to sell. According to the account given by his great-niece Lauren Templeton, the positions were closed for a profit of more than $90m, although this was a trade in his personal portfolio rather than in a fund, and the figures rest on that family account rather than on any published statement.

Irene Templeton died in 1993 and his daughter Anne in 2004. In 1999 Money magazine called him "arguably the greatest global stock picker of the century". Sir John Templeton died of pneumonia at Doctors Hospital in Nassau on 8 July 2008, aged 95, and was survived by his sons John and Christopher, a stepdaughter, three grandchildren and three great-grandchildren, while the foundation he left behind then had an endowment of around $1.5bn and was giving away about $70m a year.
