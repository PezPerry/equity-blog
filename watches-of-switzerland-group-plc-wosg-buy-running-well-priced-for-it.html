Business Model

Watches of Switzerland Group is a retailer of luxury watches and luxury jewellery in the United Kingdom and the United States. It buys from the brand owners and sells through its own showrooms and websites.

It is an authorised retailer rather than a brand owner, so the product, the recommended retail price and the amount of stock it is allocated are all set by the brands.

It trades through eight brands:

  • Watches of Switzerland: in both countries.
  • Goldsmiths and Mappin & Webb: in the United Kingdom.
  • Mayors, Betteridge and Deutsch & Deutsch: in the United States.
  • Analog:Shift and Hodinkee: the pre-owned and online watch businesses.

It also holds the exclusive distribution rights for Roberto Coin jewellery in North America, which it runs as a wholesale division.

Luxury watches were £1,507m of the £1,828m of revenue in the full year of 2026, 82% of it. Luxury jewellery was £238m and services and other revenue £83m.

Demand for the key watch brands is greater than the supply the company is given, and it runs registration of interest lists for those models. Sales of them are limited by allocation, not by the number of customers.

🎓 Clarity What does it mean that sales are "limited by allocation"? The watch brands decide how many of each model a retailer may receive, so for the most wanted references Watches of Switzerland cannot simply order more stock to sell more. It has waiting lists instead. That cuts both ways for this thesis: it supports the pricing and the lack of discounting, but it also means the brands — not the retailer — control both the volume and the margin the retailer is given, which is exactly what went against the company in 2026.

The United States became the larger half of the group in 2026, at £927m of revenue against £901m in the United Kingdom, and it earns the better margin.

🎓 Clarity What are "adjusted EBIT", a "margin" and a "basis point"? EBIT is earnings before interest and tax — the operating profit before financing costs and tax. "Adjusted" means the company has stripped out items it regards as one-off. The margin is that profit as a percentage of revenue: £154.8m on £1,828m is 8.5%. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 80 to 100 basis points of margin expansion means roughly 0.8 to 1.0 percentage points. Generally speaking, I don't pay much attention to adjusted earnings before interest and tax. The comparison does provide some clarity here though.

Roberto Coin is the one part of the group that is not retail. The company bought the North American distribution business in May 2024 and its wholesale revenue was £126.9m in 2026, up 22% at constant currency, at a far higher margin than the showrooms.

The estate was 186 showrooms at the start of September, 75 of them mono-brand boutiques run with the brands — shops that sell a single watch brand. The company has been closing smaller showrooms and spending on larger ones, with £65.9m of expansionary capital expenditure in 2026. Capital expenditure, or capex, is spending on long-lived assets such as fitting out showrooms.

Growth in the United States has also come from buying other retailers. The latest is a majority stake in Deutsch & Deutsch, four Rolex agencies in Texas, which completed in January 2026.

The company doesn't pay a dividend. It completed a £25m share buyback in June 2025, and the board has said it will decide between buybacks and dividends when there is surplus capital.

United States revenue rose 22% at constant currency to £501m. For the full year of 2026 the constant currency increase was 24%. The retail business alone was 25% and this half was sitting at 24%. That's pretty good. I continue to be happy with that, even if it is a slight reduction from the full year figure.

On the United Kingdom side, revenue has picked up slightly. Revenue was up 9% to £473m, against 5% for the full year of 2026 and 2% in the first half of it.

The company says this was against an improving market backdrop. I find it surprising that it was an improving market backdrop.

Net debt was £24m at 1 November, down from £112m a year earlier and £57m at the year end in May.

The chief executive says, and I quote: "We enter the second half well positioned for the full year, having finished the first half slightly ahead of our expectations."

We still have to remember that the market is quite volatile for the company. The macroeconomic environment has worsened in recent times.

🎓 Clarity What is "constant currency", and why does it matter here? Constant currency restates this year's foreign sales at last year's exchange rates, so you see the underlying trading rather than the movement in the pound against the dollar. It matters because roughly half of this group's revenue is earned in the United States: the reported United States figure is +23% while the constant currency figure is +22%, so about one percentage point of the headline came from currency rather than from selling more watches. At group level both numbers are +15%, so the currency effect washed out.

United States growth of 22% includes a good contribution from Deutsch & Deutsch, which has been in the figures since January and drops out of the comparison from then.

The United Kingdom has improved in each of the last three halves, from 2% to 7% to 9%, with luxury jewellery and certified pre-owned watches both outperforming.

Margin

The adjusted margin fell 60 basis points to 8.5% in the full year of 2026, from 9.1%, which the company put down to the brands lowering the margin they give retailers after the United States tariffs and the rise in the gold price, and to product mix.

This half takes back 80 to 100 basis points of it, to around 8.9% to 9.1% against 8.1% in the first half of 2026, which the company describes as operating leverage.

Debt

The company seems to be reducing its debt levels. Net debt — borrowings less cash — of £24m is down £88m in twelve months, after £161.7m of free cash flow in the full year of 2026, a conversion of 80%.

These figures are before lease liabilities. Including them, net debt was £483m at the year end in May.

🎓 Clarity Why are there two net debt figures, £24m and £483m? Free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and paying for capital expenditure; "conversion" measures how much of the adjusted profit turns into that cash — 80% in 2026. The debt gap is about leases: a retailer signs long leases on its showrooms, and accounting rules put the future rent on the balance sheet as a liability. £24m is the borrowings-less-cash figure the company guides on; £483m at the May year end includes those showroom lease obligations. Both are real — the first tells you about the bank debt, the second about the fixed costs of the estate.

Clarity on the half

Things do seem to be progressing quite nicely for Watches of Switzerland.

Revenue is up 15% in the half, 22% in the United States and 9% in the United Kingdom, and the adjusted profit is up around 28% as the margin lost in 2026 comes back.

Revenues are going in the right direction despite a relatively weak environment in both the United States and the United Kingdom, and the company is selling well in watches as well as jewellery.

Net debt is down to £24m.

In fairness, it will be their third year of significant profit increases.

However, the market is quite volatile for the company and the macroeconomic environment has worsened in recent times. The company's own guidance has the second half growing more slowly than the first.

My feelings are that this is all quite positive, but the shares seem to be quite highly valued, especially for a company that's not offering any dividends at this stage. I do expect that the market will view this quite positively overall, and I do think the market has a tendency of valuing Watches of Switzerland slightly higher than what I would naturally assume of a retailer of this sort.

Valuation

I am anticipating a 9% increase in revenue for the full year, which would bring total revenue to £1.992bn.

Given that I expect, at the very least, not a decrease in their margin levels as they consolidate this position, I estimate pre-tax profit of £157.97m, or post-tax profit of £118.48m. This gives me an earnings per share figure of 51.2p.

I'm going to give them a growth rate of 10% for the moment. That might seem a bit on the high side, but their five-year earnings per share average has been 12% growth.

I've put an uplift of 6% on my valuation because I do think things are running well.

As a result, I come up with a valuation of 781.52p per share which, on the current share price of 745p, gives us a value opportunity of +4.9%.

It is a bit of a weak buy. This isn't much of a value opportunity here.

🎓 Clarity What is an earnings override, and why is the price-to-earnings ratio on this page 14.6× rather than 16.6×? Earnings per share (EPS) is profit after tax divided by the number of shares. Here the model does not use a historic reported figure: it uses my own full-year estimate of 51.2p (override, FY2027e), built from £1.992bn of revenue, £157.97m of pre-tax profit (PBT — profit before tax is deducted) and £118.48m after tax, across 231.41m shares. The price-to-earnings ratio shown in the table below is recomputed on that same 51.2p: 745p ÷ 51.2p = 14.6×. The 16.6× carried in my model sheet is struck against a different, lower earnings base, so it does not tie to the figure the valuation is actually built on — and a ratio that uses one earnings number while the valuation uses another is the sort of thing that quietly misleads.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong

  • A volatile market for the company: We still have to remember that the market is quite volatile for the company, and the macroeconomic environment has worsened in recent times.
  • A slower second half, by the company's own guidance: The company expects lower revenue growth in the second half than in the first, with more moderate margin expansion. The second half has one fewer trading week than last year, and the guidance is on a 52 week basis against a 2026 that had 53 weeks.
  • The Deutsch & Deutsch anniversary: United States growth of 22% includes a good contribution from Deutsch & Deutsch, which has been in the figures since January and drops out of the comparison from then.
  • The brands set the margin: The adjusted margin fell 60 basis points to 8.5% in 2026, which the company put down to the brands lowering the margin they give retailers after the United States tariffs and the rise in the gold price, and to product mix. As an authorised retailer, the product, the price and the allocation are not in the company's hands.
  • No dividend: The shares seem to be quite highly valued, especially for a company that's not offering any dividends at this stage. The board has only said it will decide between buybacks and dividends when there is surplus capital.
  • A rating that may not hold: I do think the market has a tendency of valuing Watches of Switzerland slightly higher than what I would naturally assume of a retailer of this sort — and at +4.9%, this isn't much of a value opportunity here.
  • Leases behind the headline debt: Net debt of £24m is before lease liabilities. Including them, net debt was £483m at the year end in May.
  • Risk factor: 20% on my model.

What Would Change My Mind

  • A decrease in margin levels: My pre-tax profit estimate of £157.97m rests on expecting, at the very least, not a decrease in their margin levels as they consolidate this position. Any fresh margin give-back to the brands undoes the earnings base.
  • Revenue short of 9%: I am anticipating a 9% increase for the full year, to £1.992bn. The company's guidance range starts at 7% at constant currency, and the lower end of it would put my £1.992bn out of reach.
  • The United Kingdom turning back down: The United Kingdom has improved in each of the last three halves, from 2% to 7% to 9%. I find it surprising that it was an improving market backdrop, so a reversal here would not shock me.
  • The United States slowing further: This half was 22% at constant currency against 24% for the full year of 2026 — a slight reduction I'm still happy with, but the trend is the thing to watch once Deutsch & Deutsch annualises.
  • The price: At 745p the value opportunity is only +4.9%. A lower price, or a dividend being introduced, would make this a good deal more than a weak buy.

Bottom Line — Buy

Things do seem to be progressing quite nicely. Revenue is up 15% in the half, 22% in the United States and 9% in the United Kingdom, the adjusted profit is up around 28% as the margin lost in 2026 comes back, and net debt is down to £24m. In fairness, it will be their third year of significant profit increases.

My feelings are that this is all quite positive, but the shares seem to be quite highly valued, especially for a company that's not offering any dividends at this stage. On 51.2p of earnings, a 10% growth rate, an 8× multiple and a 6% uplift I get 781.52p against 745p. It is a bit of a weak buy. This isn't much of a value opportunity here.
