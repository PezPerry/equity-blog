The Shares Ran Ahead of the Copper

Half year to 30 June 2026, third-quarter production to 30 September 2026, and my full-year 2026 estimate. Currency note: Atalaya reports in euros and states earnings per share in euro cents (55.4c = €0.554); the metal is priced in US dollars a pound and the shares trade in pence. My valuation works in pence, converting the 78c of forecast full-year earnings per share to 65.43p.

Briefing

  • Thesis: A decent single-mine copper producer in a benign country with debt under control, but the share price is currently ahead of the upward movement in copper prices. It is prudent to wait for a better price.
  • The numbers: Earnings per share of 65.43p (override, FY2026e), a 12% growth rate and an 8x multiple give a formula valuation of 1,025.94p; a -10% reduction for copper's volatility takes the actual valuation to 923.35p against a current price of 904.50p, a value opportunity of +2.1%.
  • Key strength: Copper is doing the work — the realised price rose 41% to US$6.02 a pound in the first half, revenue rose 3.9% to €264.7m despite production falling -14.7%, profit after tax rose 40% to €84.0m, and net cash was €342.1m at the end of September.
  • Key risk: One producing mine in one country, output that I don't think is going to improve too dramatically, a copper price that goes all over the place, and a dividend yield of just 1%, which is a bit too low for my preference.
  • Overall stance: Hold. +2.1% isn't enough to warrant an investment; I want to see more than that. I would potentially be interested and buy shares at somewhere around 678p or under.

Business Model

Atalaya Mining is a European copper producer. It owns and operates Proyecto Riotinto, an open-pit copper mine with a 15 million tonne a year processing plant in the province of Huelva in south-west Spain, and holds a set of development and exploration projects elsewhere in Spain and in Sweden.

Atalaya mines copper ore from the Cerro Colorado open pit at Riotinto and processes it into a copper concentrate of around 17% copper, with silver as a by-product. Concentrate is the powder left after the plant has crushed the rock and floated off the copper-bearing minerals; a by-product is a second metal that comes out of the same rock and is sold alongside the main one. The concentrate is trucked to the port of Huelva and sold to smelters and traders, so revenues are in US dollars while most of the costs are in euros.

All of the revenue comes from Riotinto. The rest of the portfolio is at the permitting, drilling or earn-in stage:

  • Proyecto Riotinto: the Cerro Colorado pit and the plant, with waste stripping at San Dionisio under way to reach higher-grade ore for blending, and drilling at the San Antonio polymetallic deposit to the east.
  • Proyecto Touro: a copper project in Galicia held through Cobre San Rafael, in which Atalaya has 10% with an earn-in to 80% — an earn-in is the right to acquire a larger stake by spending an agreed amount on the ground rather than by paying cash to the owner. Declared a strategic industrial project by the Galician authorities in June 2024 and recognised as a strategic project by the European Commission in October 2026, with the environmental impact statement well advanced.
  • Masa Valverde: 100% owned, with the environmental authorisation and the exploitation permit both granted; infill drilling continues and a board decision on the access ramp is pending.
  • Riotinto East: the Peñas Blancas and Cerro Negro investigation permits covering 10,016 hectares, with targets defined and drilling due to begin.
  • Ossa Morena: 99.9% owned, where drilling at the Guijarro gold-copper project started in 2026.
  • E-LIX: a hydrometallurgical plant at Riotinto — one that dissolves metal out of the ore chemically rather than smelting it — intended to produce copper and zinc metal from the polymetallic ores. It has been running intermittently below design capacity and was impaired by €24.1m in 2025, with €31.8m still on the balance sheet.
  • Skellefte Belt and Rockliden: two earn-ins in northern Sweden signed in November 2024.
  • Lara Exploration: a 7.3% shareholding bought in April 2026.

They have an additional interest in two projects in Sweden which began in November 2024, with an initial target interest of 75%. The Swedish earn-ins are with Mineral Prospektering i Sverige AB, a private company from Malå in Västerbotten. The Skellefte Belt project covers 224 square kilometres in Västerbotten county and Rockliden covers 130 square kilometres in Västernorrland county, both in districts of volcanogenic massive sulphide deposits — sulphide-rich bodies formed on the ancient sea floor — that carry copper, zinc and lead with gold and silver.

Atalaya can earn 51% of each project by spending US$6m within four years, and 75% by spending a further US$6m and completing a scoping study, US$12m per project in all. It funded €4.3m in 2025, has elected to proceed to the first stage on the Skellefte Belt and, after the half year, earned its 51% there. 43 holes for 12,617 metres have been drilled at Skellefte and 25 holes for 4,593 metres at Rockliden.

As of April 2026, they acquired 7.3% of Lara Exploration. Lara is a Canadian exploration company listed on the Toronto Venture Exchange that runs a prospect-generator model, holding ground and bringing in partners to fund the drilling, with its projects in Brazil and Peru. Its main asset is the 100%-owned Planalto copper-gold project in the Carajás mineral province of northern Brazil. A preliminary economic assessment in October 2025 — an early, deliberately rough study of whether a mine would pay — contemplated an open pit feeding an 8 million tonne a year flotation plant, producing 560,000 tonnes of copper and 111,000 ounces of gold over an 18-year mine life. Atalaya subscribed for 4.5m shares at C$3.00 each in a private placement, C$13.5m in all, and says it holds the shares for investment purposes.

So there is danger within this company. It's spread over not very many projects, with output mostly in one single country, albeit a very stable European country.

Six Years in Numbers

2025 is the last full set of accounts we have from them. Revenue rose 47.8% to €482.9m, the highest the company has produced, with a very good gross margin of 32.0%. Gross margin is what is left of each pound of sales after the direct cost of digging and processing the ore.

This compares with significantly lower revenue the year before, when they only had €326.8m of revenue and a gross margin of only 15.1%.

The years before that saw negative turnover growth, -10.8% in 2022, -5.9% in 2023 and -4.0% in 2024, after a 60.5% rise in 2021. There is a huge amount of difference in these figures from one year to the next.

The First Half of 2026

Looking at the most recent results, the company produced less copper in the first half of 2026. This is supposedly because heavy rain in late January and early February restricted access to parts of the Cerro Colorado pit, leading the company to supplement plant feed with lower-grade stockpiles, and the head grade fell to 0.35% from 0.42%.

That's fine in itself, but I do find it tiresome when miners complain about rainfall affecting production output. That's probably a little unfair on my part, but I can't help feeling they should be able to work around standard weather issues that occur quite frequently.

Between the first half of 2025 and the first half of 2026, copper production fell -14.7% to 23,432 tonnes from 27,466 tonnes.

Nonetheless, revenue rose 3.9% to €264.7m because the price of copper has been strong. The realised copper price — the price the company actually achieved on the metal it sold, rather than the headline market price — was up 41% to US$6.02 a pound from US$4.27 a pound the year before.

Gross profit rose 20.7% to €107.8m from €89.3m. That was the copper price: contained copper sold fell -22.5% to 22,248 tonnes from 28,711 tonnes, while operating costs came down a little to €138.5m from €147.2m, so the gross profit increase came from the price.

The first half of this year saw quite strong profitability, stronger than in the first half of 2025, which was an impressive year. Profit after tax rose 40% to €84.0m from €60.1m and basic earnings per share — profit after tax divided by the number of shares, or EPS — were 55.4c against 42.7c.

🎓 Clarity How can revenue go up when the mine produced 14.7% less copper? Three things sit between rock and revenue. Head grade is how much copper is in each tonne of rock — it fell to 0.35% from 0.42%, so the same 8.2 million tonnes through the plant carried less metal. Recovery is the share of that metal the plant actually captures — it improved to 82.89% from 78.90%, clawing a little back. Then comes the price: the realised price rose 41% to US$6.02 a pound, which was more than enough to cover selling 22.5% less contained copper. That is why gross profit rose 20.7% on falling volumes, and it matters here because the earnings this valuation rests on are being driven by the copper price rather than by the mine.

Pleasingly, borrowings reduced to €28.7m from €38.6m. Net cash was €318.3m at the end of June, after a £130m equity placing in January 2026 that took the shares in issue to 153.8m from 140.8m, and €342.1m at the end of September.

I would still say that dividends are extremely low for this company. The interim went up to 5.5c from 4.4c, but the shares are yielding just 1%, which is a bit too low for my preference.

In the third quarter the company produced 13,967 tonnes, up 3.5% on the second quarter and up 15% on the third quarter of 2025, at a grade of 0.40% and a recovery of 84.25%, taking the year to date to 37,399 tonnes.

What Matters for This Company

Production and guidance

In March the company guided that second-half production would run approximately 10% above the first half. On the first half's 23,432 tonnes that implies 25,775 tonnes, and as a full-year figure around 49,200 tonnes, which would be slightly below their 50,000 to 54,000 tonne guidance range.

The company's position since the third quarter is that production will come in at the low end of the range. To reach 50,000 tonnes the fourth quarter needs 12,601 tonnes against the 13,967 tonnes produced in the third.

Shareholders

It may also be slightly concerning that in August of this year their largest strategic shareholder, Trafigura, sold out of its shareholding. Trafigura's Urion Investment Holdings placed 16.8m shares, 10.94% of the company, at 915p a share through a J.P. Morgan bookbuild on 12 August 2026, raising around £154m. A bookbuild is the process of selling a large block of shares to institutions in a day, usually at a discount to the market price.

915p is slightly higher than the current share price of 904.50p.

Trafigura had been with the company since 2015, as a shareholder, as the offtaker for part of the concentrate and, through Impala Terminals, as the handler of the concentrate at Huelva. An offtaker is the buyer that has contracted in advance to take the mine's output. In 2018 it held 22%, alongside the Chinese smelter Yanggu Xiangguang Copper, which also held 22% and left the register in 2022.

Another large shareholder, Cobas Asset Management, reduced its shareholding to 4.6% from 9.89% in April 2026. The largest shareholder on the register in August was Ithaki SAS at 8.3%.

Copper prices

The last couple of years have been quite dramatic for copper pricing. Back in November 2023 copper was at 369.7 US cents a pound on the open market, whereas now it sits at 648, so some dramatic upward movement, and no doubt still helping the company even though the production volumes are not particularly great at this point in time.

For this year thus far, copper has gone from around 580 cents to the current 648, a rise of around 12%, with a record of 689 cents on 9 September.

I believe the thesis lies in the move to lower carbon emissions, the build-out of artificial intelligence data centres and, generally, the increase in compute power required across the world, which has been driving up copper prices over the last couple of years and no doubt helps the company.

Since the start of 2016, when copper was around US$2.10 a pound, the price has risen around 210%, a compound rate of around 11% a year, although there has been a lot of volatility along the way. I won't be shocked to see further upward pressure on copper prices, which can further assist the company.

Global refined copper demand was around 28 million tonnes in 2025. The International Energy Agency expects demand from energy transition technologies, which means grids, electric vehicles and renewables, to more than triple by the mid-2030s, and says existing and planned mines can meet only around 70% of projected 2035 demand.

Data centres are the newer part of the story. An artificial intelligence data centre needs around 27 to 47 tonnes of copper per megawatt of capacity, so a one-gigawatt campus can absorb up to 50,000 tonnes, about a year of Atalaya's output. Sprott and CRU both put data centre copper demand at around 1.1 million tonnes a year by 2030, close to 3% of global demand, and Wood Mackenzie adds that up to a further 5 million tonnes could go into the transmission and distribution networks feeding them.

Supply is the other half. Wood Mackenzie put the refined market in a 304,000 tonne deficit in 2025. The International Copper Study Group has swung its 2026 forecast from a surplus of more than 200,000 tonnes to a deficit of around 150,000 tonnes, and UBS sees a deficit of more than 400,000 tonnes. The Grasberg mud rush in Indonesia has taken more than 500,000 tonnes out of the market through 2026, ore grades across the industry have fallen by around 40% since 1991, and a new greenfield mine takes around 17 years from discovery to production.

Where the copper goes

Riotinto is 70 kilometres by road from the port of Huelva, and the concentrate goes out by sea from there. Impala Terminals, a Trafigura company, handles, stores and ships it under a five-year agreement renewed in December 2023 for around €12m of fees over its life.

The concentrate is sold under offtake agreements and spot tenders. The offtake agreements signed in 2015 committed the whole of the forecast production to large shareholders of the time, Trafigura and the Chinese smelter Yanggu Xiangguang Copper, and 10 of the 19 spot sales between 2015 and 2023 also went to Trafigura, so a good part of the concentrate has historically been traded on to Asian smelters rather than consumed in Europe.

The copper price itself is a world price set in London and New York, so mining in Europe earns no premium on the metal. The advantage is on the cost side. A mine 70 kilometres from a deep-water port at low altitude hauls its concentrate for a fraction of what the Andean mines pay to bring theirs down from 3,000 to 4,000 metres, and Huelva sits across the water from Europe's own smelters, Atlantic Copper at the port itself, Aurubis in Germany and Bulgaria and Boliden in Sweden and Finland.

Freight, treatment charges and other offsite costs were US$0.25 a pound payable in 2025 against US$0.58 in 2024, although most of that drop was the collapse in smelter treatment charges, with the annual benchmark falling to US$21.25 a tonne for 2025 from US$80 in 2024 as the smelters chased scarce concentrate. A treatment charge is the fee the smelter deducts for turning concentrate into metal, so when it falls the miner keeps more of the copper price.

Being in Spain also brings a stable permitting regime and European Union support. Touro was recognised as a strategic project by the European Commission in October 2026, which should help the environmental permitting.

Against that, Spain means European energy prices. The plant uses around 22.6 kilowatt hours of electricity per tonne processed, which is why the company has built a 50 megawatt solar plant and taken out a long-term power purchase agreement, and higher electricity costs were one of the drivers of the 2025 cost of sales. The ore grade of 0.39% is low by world standards, so cash costs of US$2.40 a pound in 2025 sit in the middle of the industry rather than at the bottom.

Clarity on the position

They operate in a relatively benign environment, Spain, as opposed to predominantly South America.

I don't think their output is going to improve too dramatically, but I see their debt levels are under control.

I think that copper prices are likely to continue to rise at very high rates in the coming years, and as a result I'm going to assume that their gross margins over the long term are going to outperform potentially higher cost rates, i.e. higher energy costs.

The share price has moved around the place over the years. Overlaying copper prices, at certain points in time the share price has massively outstripped copper prices, and at other times it has not. I think the company's share price is currently ahead of the upward movement in copper prices.

Indexed to November 2016, the shares are up 550% against 150% for copper. Expressed as the share price in pence for each US dollar of copper, the ten-year average is 84p, the 2021 and 2024 peaks were 96p and 99p, and today it is 136p, having touched 174p in January 2026 when the shares were 1,028p.

🎓 Clarity What does “136p per dollar of copper” actually measure? Take the share price in pence and divide it by the copper price in US dollars a pound. It is a crude way of asking how much the market is paying for the shares relative to the metal that drives their earnings. Over ten years that ratio has averaged 84p; it peaked at 96p in 2021 and 99p in 2024; today, with the shares at 904.50p and copper around US$6.55, it is 136p. That is the core of the thesis here: copper has risen a long way, but the shares have risen further, which is why I think the price is ahead of the metal and why I would rather wait.

My feelings are that I think it is prudent to wait for a better price. I would potentially be interested in the company and buy shares, but what I would be looking for is a share price of somewhere around 678p or under to be purchasing them.

Valuation

On the basis of the current half-year figures, which came in at 55.4c per share, my expectation is for the full year to hit €120m of post-tax profit, which equates to 78c per share, or 65.43p.

I'm giving a growth rate of 12%, which gives a formula valuation of 1,025.94p.

I'm also giving a reduction of -10% because of the issues with dealing with copper and the volatile nature of its prices. They don't just go up all the time, year after year; they go all over the place. It's just that over the last ten years they have averaged a rise of around 12% a year.

This gives an actual valuation of 923.35p per share, a value opportunity of +2.1%. This isn't enough to warrant an investment; I want to see more than that, so I'm pitching it as a hold.

🎓 Clarity Why is the earnings figure a forecast rather than last year's reported number? The model uses an earnings override: instead of the 58.3c of diluted earnings per share Atalaya reported for 2025, or the 55.4c basic figure reported for the first half of 2026, it uses 65.43p — my estimate of full-year 2026 earnings, built from €120m of expected post-tax profit across 153.76m shares, which is 78c a share converted into pence. An override is used when the trailing figure no longer describes the business you are buying; here the first half alone already earned 55.4c, so last year's 58.3c would understate the run rate. Everything in the snapshot below — the price-to-earnings ratio, the formula valuation, the value opportunity — is therefore calculated on forecast 2026 earnings, not on reported history.

🎓 Clarity How does the formula valuation get from 65.43p of earnings to 923.35p a share? The model takes earnings per share, multiplies by the growth rate and the multiple to get the value of the growth (65.43p × 12% × 8 = 62.81p), adds the earnings back (128.24p), then applies the multiple again (× 8 = 1,025.94p). That is the formula valuation. The uplift or reduction is the judgement layer on top: here a -10% reduction for copper's volatility, which takes it to 923.35p. The value opportunity is simply that valuation divided by the current price, less one — 923.35p ÷ 904.50p − 1 = +2.1%. A big multiple does a lot of work in this arithmetic, which is why the 8x choice and the -10% haircut matter as much as the earnings estimate.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong

  • The copper price does the earning: the first half's profit growth came from a 41% rise in the realised price, not from the mine. Copper prices don't just go up all the time, year after year; they go all over the place. That volatility is exactly why I applied the -10% reduction, and it is the single biggest swing factor in the 923.35p valuation.
  • One mine, one country: there is danger within this company. All of the revenue comes from Riotinto, it's spread over not very many projects, and output is mostly in one single country — albeit a very stable European country.
  • Output isn't improving much: production fell -14.7% in the first half, the head grade fell to 0.35% from 0.42%, the company now expects the low end of its 50,000 to 54,000 tonne range, and the March guidance of second-half production around 10% above the first half implies roughly 49,200 tonnes — slightly below the range. I don't think their output is going to improve too dramatically.
  • Weather as an explanation: I do find it tiresome when miners complain about rainfall affecting production output. Probably a little unfair on my part, but standard weather issues occur quite frequently and ought to be worked around.
  • Costs held high: the cost guidance of US$2.60 to US$2.90 cash cost and US$3.10 to US$3.40 all-in sustaining cost was kept even though the first half came in below it, with the company pointing to diesel and explosives prices from the conflicts in the Middle East and Ukraine. European energy prices and a 0.39% ore grade keep Atalaya in the middle of the cost curve, not at the bottom.
  • The register is turning over: Trafigura's Urion placed 16.8m shares, 10.94% of the company, at 915p on 12 August 2026 after a decade on the register, and Cobas cut its holding to 4.6% from 9.89% in April 2026. 915p is slightly higher than the current 904.50p.
  • E-LIX: the hydrometallurgical plant has been running intermittently below design capacity, was impaired by €24.1m in 2025 and still carries €31.8m on the balance sheet.
  • The income is thin: dividends are extremely low for this company. The interim went up to 5.5c from 4.4c, but the shares yield just 1%, which is a bit too low for my preference.
  • The price is ahead of the metal: at 136p of share price per US dollar of copper against a ten-year average of 84p and prior peaks of 96p and 99p, the shares are currently ahead of the upward movement in copper prices.

What Would Change My Mind

  • A share price around 678p or under: I would potentially be interested in the company and buy shares, and that is the level I would be looking for.
  • The copper ratio normalising: copper pushing higher while the shares stand still would bring the 136p per dollar back towards the 84p ten-year average and do the same job as a lower price.
  • Output genuinely improving: a fourth quarter and a 2027 that put production in the upper half of guidance on grades back at 0.39% or better, rather than the low end, would change my view that output isn't going to improve too dramatically.
  • Costs coming in where the first half suggested: full-year cash cost and all-in sustaining cost landing nearer the first half's US$2.43 and US$2.97 than the guided ranges.
  • A dividend worth having: a payout that takes the yield meaningfully above 1%, given net cash of €342.1m at the end of September and borrowings down to €28.7m.
  • The pipeline becoming real: Touro moving on from its 10% earn-in stage with European Commission strategic status behind it, or a board decision on the Masa Valverde access ramp, would start to answer the single-asset objection.

Bottom Line — Hold

This is a company I like the shape of. Riotinto sits in a relatively benign environment, Spain, as opposed to predominantly South America; debt levels are under control, with borrowings down to €28.7m and net cash of €342.1m at the end of September; and I think copper prices are likely to continue to rise at very high rates in the coming years, which is why I'm assuming gross margins outperform potentially higher cost rates over the long term.

The problem is the price, not the business. The share price is currently ahead of the upward movement in copper prices, output isn't going to improve too dramatically, and the yield of 1% is a bit too low for my preference. On 65.43p of forecast earnings, a 12% growth rate, an 8x multiple and a -10% reduction for copper's volatility, I get 923.35p against 904.50p.
