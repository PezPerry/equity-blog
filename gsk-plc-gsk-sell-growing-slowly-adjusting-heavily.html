Growing Slowly, Adjusting Heavily

Full-year 2025 results, year ended 31 December 2025. GSK reports in pounds sterling; share price and earnings per share are quoted in pence (1,817.50p = £18.175 per share).

Briefing

  • Thesis: A safe, high-margin pharmaceutical business that is already very large and struggling to grow sales anywhere near fast enough to justify the current share price — and doing far too much adjusting to its income statement along the way.
  • The numbers: Group turnover £32,667m, up 4.1%. Adjusted earnings per share 172.0p, the highest on record; reported earnings per share 138.8p. I'm valuing on a new earnings per share figure of 113.5p, giving a valuation of 1,411.03p against a price of 1,817.50p — a value opportunity of −22.4%.
  • Key strength: Specialty Medicines, up from £11,810m to £13,474m, with oncology +43% and respiratory, immunology and inflammation +18% at constant currency, on a 72.4% gross margin.
  • Key risk: The loss of exclusivity on dolutegravir — the molecule inside most of a £7,687m HIV portfolio — starting to bite from late this decade.
  • Overall stance: I'm quite happy with the company and what it's been doing, but I don't like how much adjusting is happening to the income statement, and sales aren't progressing as fast as is warranted at the current share price. Sell.

Business Model

GSK, formerly GlaxoSmithKline, is a pharmaceutical company and one of the largest in the world, with a market capitalisation of £72.8bn — that is, the total value the stock market puts on all its shares at once. It develops medicines and vaccines to treat and prevent disease, with a focus on the immune system and advanced technologies.

The company comes up with medicines and vaccines to help treat diseases, split across three core divisions:

  • Specialty Medicines: HIV, oncology (cancer), respiratory, immunology and inflammation — treating conditions from severe asthma and cancer to HIV and autoimmune diseases such as lupus.
  • General Medicines: the more mature part of the business, holding a broad range of applications from inhalers for asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease through to established antibiotics and older medicines.
  • Vaccines: self-explanatory — a large range targeting infectious diseases including meningitis, shingles, respiratory syncytial virus, hepatitis and many more.

The big area of recent growth has been Specialty Medicines, which grew from £11.8bn of sales in 2024 to £13.5bn in 2025 while the other two divisions were broadly flat.

Commercial Operations made £16.3bn of operating profit in 2025 (2024: £15.3bn) before centrally funded research and development costs of −£6.3bn.

In a general sense the company is seeing rises in turnover, but they're quite lackadaisical. Total sales rose 3.4% in 2023, 3.5% in 2024 and 4.1% in 2025. What we can safely take from that and the long-run turnover trajectory is that the company is already very large and struggling to increase sales anywhere near fast enough to meaningfully outperform, for instance, inflation.

Profitability does move around a little and is growing. 2024 was a very bad profitability year, driven by significant legal charges and remeasurements of the contingent consideration owed on ViiV Healthcare — the adjustments table further down sets this out. In 2025 there was a rebound, with adjusted earnings per share reaching 172.0p, its highest on record. The gross margin has been increasing gently over the last couple of years and reached 72.4% in 2025, which is very high.

🎓 Clarity What is a gross margin, and why does 72.4% matter? Gross margin is what's left of each £1 of sales after the direct cost of making the product — so GSK keeps about 72p of every £1 before it pays for research, sales teams and head office. That is very high, and it's why the company can afford to spend 23% of turnover on research. It also means the argument here is not about profitability at all: it's about whether sales are growing fast enough to justify the price being asked for the shares.

What Matters for This Company

Specialty Medicines — the growth, and the cliff under it

Specialty Medicines is where the growth is, and the company expects that to continue — its 2026 planning assumption is for low double-digit percentage growth in the division. Respiratory, immunology and inflammation sales rose 18% at constant currency to £3.8bn, led by Nucala and Benlysta. Oncology rose 43% to £2.0bn, led by Jemperli (+89%) and Ojjaara (+60%), both still early in their launches. HIV rose 11%, with the growth coming from the newer products: the long-acting injectables Cabenuva (+42%) and Apretude (+62%) now account for 30% of HIV sales in the United States.

The main thing to be aware of in this division is the loss of exclusivity on dolutegravir, the molecule inside most of the HIV portfolio, which starts to bite from late this decade. The company frames its medium-term margin expectations "through dolutegravir loss of exclusivity", so management is planning around it, but it's a large patent cliff sitting under the biggest franchise in the group. Pipeline risk is also there: the belrestotug cancer programme was terminated in 2025 and brought a £471m impairment charge.

🎓 Clarity What is "loss of exclusivity", or a patent cliff? A new medicine is protected by patents for a fixed period, during which only the inventor can sell it. When that protection expires, rival manufacturers launch copies — generics — usually at a fraction of the price, and sales of the original can fall away very quickly. Dolutegravir sits inside most of a £7,687m HIV portfolio, which is 23.5% of everything GSK sells. That's the single biggest reason not to extrapolate today's record earnings indefinitely into the future.

Vaccines — strong abroad, weak at home

Vaccines is performing well outside the United States: Shingrix sales in Europe rose 42% following the French launch, Japan accelerated after expanded reimbursement, and markets outside the United States now represent 66% of global Shingrix sales, up from 56% in 2024. The company puts the average immunisation rate across the ten biggest of those markets at around 10%. Meningitis vaccines rose 12% and the newly approved Penmenvy has started selling. The pipeline includes a pneumococcal programme and an mRNA seasonal flu programme.

The United States is the weak side. Shingrix sales there fell −17% as the pace of penetration into harder-to-activate unvaccinated consumers slowed, Arexvy fell in the American market, and influenza vaccine sales dropped −24%. The company's 2026 assumption is for the division overall to be stable to slightly down.

General Medicines — mature, cash-generative

General Medicines is the mature part of the business. Trelegy grew 13% to £2,986m and continues to take share, while the older respiratory brands (Seretide, Flixotide, Ventolin) and established antibiotics decline gently. The division as a whole fell −4% at actual rates in 2025 and the company assumes stable to slightly down for 2026. It throws off cash and requires little investment.

Research and development spend has continued to go up, reaching £7.5bn in 2025 — 23% of turnover, a step up from the roughly 20% level of the previous four years. I'm potentially okay with that because the company really does have to do a huge amount of research to ensure it comes up with new products for the future. Part of the 2025 increase was the £471m belrestotug impairment rather than live spending; core research investment was £6.6bn.

Where the money is going, so far as the company breaks it down:

  • Oncology: accelerated work on antibody-drug conjugates (the B7-H3 and B7-H4 programmes) and IDRX-42, a treatment for gastrointestinal stromal tumours acquired in early 2025.
  • Specialty Medicines: efimosfermin (a liver disease asset acquired from Boston Pharmaceuticals in 2025), bepirovirsen for hepatitis B, and the long-acting HIV treatment and prevention programmes.
  • Vaccines: the pneumococcal and mRNA seasonal flu clinical programmes.

The new chief executive

The company is transitioning to a new chief executive and has selected Luke Miels, who took over from Dame Emma Walmsley on 1 January 2026. Walmsley left the board at the end of 2025 and remains employed until September 2026 to support the handover.

Miels is Australian, 50 at the time of the appointment, with a biology degree from Flinders University and a Master of Business Administration from Macquarie University. Miels started as a sales representative at AstraZeneca in 1995, spent 2000 to 2006 at Sanofi-Aventis in strategy, country management (managing director of Aventis Thailand) and United States sales roles, then eight years at Roche, first running global marketing for metabolism and anaemia from Switzerland and then as regional head for Asia-Pacific, based in Shanghai and Singapore. Miels returned to AstraZeneca in 2014 as executive vice president for Europe and global product strategy, and joined GSK in September 2017 as chief commercial officer under Walmsley, running the global medicines and vaccines portfolio.

The cost base

Turning to the financial statements within the annual accounts: cost of sales has been kept under control, which helped boost gross profit. Turnover rose a little while cost of sales was flat — £9,017m in 2025 against £9,048m in 2024 — actually slightly down.

Selling, general and administrative costs came down to £9,088m from £11,015m in 2024. Most of that fall is the 2024 significant legal charges (chiefly Zantac-related) dropping out rather than underlying cost cutting — on the company's core basis, selling costs were flat at £9.0bn — but either way the cost base outside research is not growing.

The 2024 dip in reported profitability came from two places: £1.6bn of significant legal charges, largely the Zantac litigation settlements, and a £1.9bn transaction-related charge, mostly the remeasurement of the contingent consideration GSK owes Shionogi on the ViiV Healthcare joint venture. Both were much smaller in 2025, and 2025 also benefited from £367m of settlement income from CureVac on the mRNA patent dispute. The recurring adjustments — around £0.8bn to £1.0bn a year of amortisation on acquired intangibles plus the ViiV contingent consideration unwind — are a permanent feature of the adjusted numbers and worth keeping in mind when using the 172.0p figure.

🎓 Clarity Reported earnings per share is 138.8p and adjusted is 172.0p — which one is real? Earnings per share (EPS) is the group's profit divided by the number of shares, so it's the slice of profit attaching to one share. Reported EPS follows the accounting rules exactly. Adjusted (or "core") EPS strips out items management considers not part of normal trading — legal settlements, restructuring, and amortisation, which is the annual write-down of the cost of drug rights bought from other companies. The gap between the two is 33.2p a share here, and the reason it matters is that a chunk of it recurs every single year. The valuation below deliberately uses neither figure: it uses a third, lower one built from reported profit with an ongoing allowance for those recurring items.

Finance costs and debt

Finance costs aren't negligible — they came in at £701m gross (£532m net of finance income) — but they don't seem particularly high for the size of the balance sheet, and there wasn't a huge amount to highlight from the finance expense note.

Long-term borrowings have remained pretty stable over the last year and currently sit at £14,708m (2024: £14,637m). Looking at the long-term debt bonds, the notes are at low coupon costs on the whole — the coupon being the fixed annual interest rate GSK pays on each bond. The exceptions are the longer-dated instruments: a 6.375% sterling medium-term note maturing 2039 (£627m) and a 6.375% United States dollar medium-term note maturing 2038, which at £2.0bn of carrying value is a bit on the high side on its own. Elsewhere coupons run from 1.625% to around 5.375%, presumably because much of the book was taken out a while ago in a cheaper rate environment.

There is a danger here into the longer term. As the cheaper notes start rolling over into a higher interest rate environment, the company might see a slight rise in its long-term debt costs.

Valuation

In an overall sense I'm quite happy with the company and what it's been doing. Profitability came in very high last year at 172.0p of adjusted earnings per share, but I don't think we can assume earnings will continue at such a high growth rate, because I don't see a particular marker here to suggest sales can increase that dramatically. There's always a hope factor with pharmaceuticals that another miracle-type drug might explode revenue further, but I'm not sure GSK is on the cusp of this — maybe I'm wrong about that.

Bearing this in mind, I think it's only sensible to use a growth rate of about 6%, and that's what I'm using. I think GSK is quite a safe company and reasonably well protected in difficult markets, so I'm giving it an uplift of 5% on the valuation.

I've changed the valuation metric, and instead of the company's adjusted 172.0p I'm using a new earnings per share figure, taking reported profit before tax and assuming the following adjustments into the future:

🎓 Clarity What does "(override, FY2026e)" mean next to the earnings per share? My model normally takes the earnings per share the company itself publishes. Here I've overridden it: I've started from 2025 reported profit before tax of £7,401m, subtracted an allowance for the adjustments I expect to keep recurring (£780m of amortisation, £450m of impairment, £109m of restructuring), taxed the £6,062.0m remainder to £4,546.5m, and divided by 4,005.61m shares to get 113.5p. "FY2026e" means it's a forward estimate for the year ahead, not a published historical figure. Everything downstream — the valuation, the price-to-earnings ratio in the snapshot, the value opportunity — runs off that 113.5p, which is why the price-to-earnings ratio below reads 16.0 rather than the 10.6 you get on the company's 172.0p.

Given the new earnings figure of 113.5p per share, the value opportunity at the current share price comes out at −22.4%. Ultimately I don't think there's a huge amount of risk with GSK — the company seems very stable, and I imagine it'll continue with slow increases in its profitability — but I don't like how much adjusting is happening to the income statement, and sales aren't progressing as fast as is warranted at the current share price. The upside potential isn't that wonderful at this stage.

🎓 Clarity How does the "value opportunity" work? The model takes earnings per share, grows it by the growth rate multiplied by the valuation multiple, adds that to the earnings, then applies the multiple again: ((113.50 × 0.06 × 8) + 113.50) × 8 = 1,343.84p. The 5% uplift for a safe, well-protected business takes that to 1,411.03p. The value opportunity is simply that valuation divided by the market price, minus one: 1,411.03 ÷ 1,817.50 − 1 = −22.4%. A negative number means the shares are trading above what the model says they're worth — the market is asking 22.4% more than my valuation supports.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Sell case)

This is a Sell on price, not on quality, so the risk is that the business earns its way into the valuation faster than I've allowed. The bull case, put fairly:

  • The hope factor is real: there's always a hope with pharmaceuticals that another miracle-type drug might explode revenue further. I'm not sure GSK is on the cusp of this — maybe I'm wrong about that. If it is, a 6% growth rate is far too low.
  • Specialty Medicines is compounding fast: up from £11,810m to £13,474m, with oncology +43%, respiratory, immunology and inflammation +18% at constant currency, and the company guiding to low double-digit percentage growth in the division for 2026. Jemperli (+89%) and Ojjaara (+60%) are both still early in their launches.
  • Shingrix is barely penetrated abroad: the average immunisation rate across the ten biggest markets outside the United States is around 10%, Europe rose 42% on the French launch, and Japan accelerated after expanded reimbursement. Non-US is now 66% of Shingrix sales, up from 56%.
  • The margin is very high and the cost base isn't growing: a 72.4% gross margin, cost of sales slightly down year on year, and core selling costs flat at £9.0bn. More sales would drop through hard.
  • Long-acting injectables are working: Cabenuva (+42%) and Apretude (+62%) are now 30% of United States HIV sales, which is exactly the kind of product shift that softens the dolutegravir cliff.
  • The adjustments could genuinely fade: if the transaction-related and legal charges really are behind the group, reported profit converges towards adjusted and my 113.5p is too conservative.

Against that, the risks that support the Sell: the dolutegravir loss of exclusivity sitting under a £7,687m franchise from late this decade; the United States vaccine weakness, with Shingrix −17%, Arexvy down and influenza −24%; pipeline failure of the belrestotug type, which cost £471m in one charge; and cheaper bonds rolling over into a higher interest rate environment, which might lift long-term debt costs. The risk factor on this one is 20%.

What Would Change My Mind

  • A marker for faster sales: I don't see a particular marker here to suggest sales can increase dramatically. Group turnover growth stepping clearly above the 3.4% / 3.5% / 4.1% run rate of the last three years would let me lift the 6% growth rate.
  • Less adjusting: the gap between 138.8p reported and 172.0p adjusted narrowing, with the amortisation and ViiV contingent consideration unwind genuinely shrinking, would raise the earnings figure I'm prepared to value on above 113.5p.
  • A miracle-type drug: a pipeline asset that visibly explodes revenue — the oncology antibody-drug conjugates, efimosfermin, bepirovirsen or the mRNA flu programme delivering at scale.
  • A visible dolutegravir replacement: the long-acting injectables taking enough of the HIV base that the late-decade patent cliff stops being the biggest single number in the group.
  • The price: at 1,411.03p the shares are worth what the model says. The whole objection here is 1,817.50p, not the business.

Bottom Line — Sell

In an overall sense I'm quite happy with the company and what it's been doing. GSK is quite a safe company, reasonably well protected in difficult markets, with a 72.4% gross margin, a cost base that isn't growing, and one division — Specialty Medicines — genuinely compounding. I don't think there's a huge amount of risk here, and I imagine it'll continue with slow increases in its profitability.

But the company is already very large and struggling to increase sales anywhere near fast enough to meaningfully outperform inflation, I don't like how much adjusting is happening to the income statement, and sales aren't progressing as fast as is warranted at the current share price. On 113.5p of earnings, 6% growth, an 8x multiple and a 5% uplift for safety, I get 1,411.03p against a market price of 1,817.50p. The upside potential isn't that wonderful at this stage.

Sell. Value opportunity: −22.4%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: A.
