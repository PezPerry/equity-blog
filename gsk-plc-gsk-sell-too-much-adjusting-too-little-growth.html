Too Much Adjusting, Too Little Growth

GSK plc, full year 2025 (year ended 31 December 2025). GSK reports in sterling, so no currency conversion is applied here; earnings per share and the share price are quoted in pence, where 113.5p means £1.135 per share.

Briefing

  • Thesis: A very large, very stable pharmaceutical business that I'm quite happy with — but sales are growing lackadaisically, the income statement is being adjusted heavily, and the share price already assumes more than that.
  • The numbers: Group turnover £32,667m in 2025, up 4.1%. Adjusted earnings per share 172.0p, the highest on record; reported diluted earnings per share 138.8p. I value on my own 113.5p figure instead, which at 6% growth, an 8x multiple and a 5% uplift gives 1,411.03p against a 1,817.50p share price — a value opportunity of -22.4%.
  • Key strength: Specialty Medicines, which grew from £11,810m of sales in 2024 to £13,474m in 2025 while the other two divisions were broadly flat, with oncology up 43% and Shingrix building well outside the United States.
  • Key risk: Loss of exclusivity on dolutegravir, the molecule inside most of an HIV portfolio that is 23.5% of group turnover, starting to bite from late this decade.
  • Overall stance: Sell. Not much risk in the company, but not much upside in the price either. Research grade A, probability 50%.

Business Model

GSK, formerly GlaxoSmithKline, is a pharmaceutical company and one of the largest in the world, with a market capitalisation of £72.8bn. It develops medicines and vaccines to treat and prevent disease, with a focus on the immune system and advanced technologies.

The company comes up with medicines and vaccines to help treat diseases. Specialty Medicines treats conditions from severe asthma and cancer to HIV and autoimmune diseases such as lupus. General Medicines holds a broad range of applications, from inhalers for asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease through to antibiotics. The Vaccines division has a large range targeting infectious diseases including meningitis, shingles, respiratory syncytial virus, hepatitis and many more.

There are three core divisions. Specialty Medicines covers HIV, oncology, respiratory, immunology and inflammation. General Medicines is the more mature part of the business, covering established respiratory brands and older medicines. The Vaccines division is self-explanatory.

Commercial Operations made £16.3bn of operating profit in 2025 (2024: £15.3bn) before centrally funded research and development costs of -£6.3bn.

In a general sense the company is seeing rises in turnover, but they're quite lackadaisical. Total sales rose 3.4% in 2023, 3.5% in 2024 and 4.1% in 2025. What we can safely take from that and the long-run turnover trajectory is that the company is already very large and struggling to increase sales anywhere near fast enough to meaningfully outperform, for instance, inflation.

Profitability does move around a little and is growing. 2024 was a very bad profitability year, driven by significant legal charges and remeasurements of the contingent consideration owed on ViiV Healthcare — the adjustments table further down sets this out. In 2025 there was a rebound, with adjusted earnings per share reaching 172.0p, its highest on record. The gross margin — turnover less the direct cost of making the product, as a percentage of turnover — has been increasing gently over the last couple of years and reached 72.4% in 2025, which is very high.

🎓 Clarity What is the difference between "actual rates" and "constant currency"? Actual rates are what the company really banked once foreign sales were converted into sterling, so a strong or weak pound moves the number. Constant currency strips the exchange-rate effect out by using the same rates in both years, showing only the underlying volume and price movement. It matters here because roughly half of GSK's turnover is American: the divisional growth I quote at constant currency (Specialty respiratory up 18%, oncology up 43%) is the trading performance, while the -4% fall in General Medicines at actual rates is what actually landed in the group accounts.

What Matters for This Company

Each division in depth

Specialty Medicines is where the growth is, and the company expects that to continue — its 2026 planning assumption is for low double-digit percentage growth in the division. Respiratory, immunology and inflammation sales rose 18% at constant currency to £3.8bn, led by Nucala and Benlysta. Oncology rose 43% to £2.0bn, led by Jemperli (+89%) and Ojjaara (+60%), both still early in their launches. HIV rose 11%, with the growth coming from the newer products: the long-acting injectables Cabenuva (+42%) and Apretude (+62%) now account for 30% of HIV sales in the United States.

The main thing to be aware of in this division is the loss of exclusivity on dolutegravir, the molecule inside most of the HIV portfolio, which starts to bite from late this decade. The company frames its medium-term margin expectations "through dolutegravir loss of exclusivity", so management is planning around it, but it's a large patent cliff sitting under the biggest franchise in the group. Pipeline risk is also there: the belrestotug cancer programme was terminated in 2025 and brought a £471m impairment charge.

🎓 Clarity What is a "loss of exclusivity" or "patent cliff"? A new medicine is protected by patents for a fixed period, during which only the inventor can sell it. When that protection expires, generic manufacturers copy the molecule and sell it far cheaper, and the original product's sales can fall away in a couple of years rather than a couple of decades. Dolutegravir sits inside most of GSK's HIV portfolio, and HIV is 23.5% of group turnover — so this is not a footnote risk, it is the single largest revenue block in the company facing a known expiry date late this decade. It is one reason I won't pay a growth price today.

Vaccines is performing well outside the United States: Shingrix sales in Europe rose 42% following the French launch, Japan accelerated after expanded reimbursement, and markets outside the United States now represent 66% of global Shingrix sales, up from 56% in 2024. The company puts the average immunisation rate across the ten biggest of those markets at around 10%. Meningitis vaccines rose 12% and the newly approved Penmenvy has started selling. The pipeline includes a pneumococcal programme and an mRNA seasonal flu programme.

The United States is the weak side. Shingrix sales there fell -17% as the pace of penetration into harder-to-activate unvaccinated consumers slowed, Arexvy fell in the American market, and influenza vaccine sales dropped -24%. The company's 2026 assumption is for the division overall to be stable to slightly down.

General Medicines is the mature part of the business. Trelegy grew 13% to £2,986m and continues to take share, while the older respiratory brands (Seretide, Flixotide, Ventolin) and established antibiotics decline gently. The division as a whole fell -4% at actual rates in 2025 and the company assumes stable to slightly down for 2026. It throws off cash and requires little investment.

Research and development spend has continued to go up, reaching £7.5bn in 2025 — 23% of turnover, a step up from the roughly 20% level of the previous four years. I'm potentially okay with that because the company really does have to do a huge amount of research to ensure it comes up with new products for the future. Part of the 2025 increase was the £471m belrestotug impairment rather than live spending; core research investment was £6.6bn.

Where the money is going, so far as the company breaks it down: in oncology, accelerated work on antibody-drug conjugates (the B7-H3 and B7-H4 programmes) and IDRX-42, a treatment for gastrointestinal stromal tumours acquired in early 2025; in Specialty Medicines, efimosfermin (a liver disease asset acquired from Boston Pharmaceuticals in 2025), bepirovirsen for hepatitis B, and the long-acting HIV treatment and prevention programmes; and in Vaccines, the pneumococcal and mRNA seasonal flu clinical programmes.

The new chief executive

The company is transitioning to a new chief executive and has selected Luke Miels, who took over from Dame Emma Walmsley on 1 January 2026. Walmsley left the board at the end of 2025 and remains employed until September 2026 to support the handover.

Miels is Australian, 50 at the time of the appointment, with a biology degree from Flinders University and a Master of Business Administration from Macquarie University. Miels started as a sales representative at AstraZeneca in 1995, spent 2000 to 2006 at Sanofi-Aventis in strategy, country management (managing director of Aventis Thailand) and United States sales roles, then eight years at Roche, first running global marketing for metabolism and anaemia from Switzerland and then as regional head for Asia-Pacific, based in Shanghai and Singapore. Miels returned to AstraZeneca in 2014 as executive vice president for Europe and global product strategy, and joined GSK in September 2017 as chief commercial officer under Walmsley, running the global medicines and vaccines portfolio.

The cost base

Turning to the financial statements within the annual accounts: cost of sales has been kept under control, which helped boost gross profit. Turnover rose a little while cost of sales was flat — £9,017m in 2025 against £9,048m in 2024, actually slightly down.

Selling, general and administrative costs came down to £9,088m from £11,015m in 2024. Most of that fall is the 2024 significant legal charges (chiefly Zantac-related) dropping out rather than underlying cost cutting — on the company's core basis, selling costs were flat at £9.0bn — but either way the cost base outside research is not growing.

The 2024 dip in reported profitability came from two places: £1.6bn of significant legal charges, largely the Zantac litigation settlements, and a £1.9bn transaction-related charge, mostly the remeasurement of the contingent consideration GSK owes Shionogi on the ViiV Healthcare joint venture. Both were much smaller in 2025, and 2025 also benefited from £367m of settlement income from CureVac on the mRNA patent dispute.

The recurring adjustments — around £0.8bn to £1.0bn a year of amortisation on acquired intangibles plus the ViiV contingent consideration unwind — are a permanent feature of the adjusted numbers and worth keeping in mind when using the 172.0p figure.

Finance costs and debt

Finance costs aren't negligible — they came in at £701m gross (£532m net of finance income) — but they don't seem particularly high for the size of the balance sheet, and there wasn't a huge amount to highlight from the finance expense note.

Long-term borrowings have remained pretty stable over the last year and currently sit at £14,708m (2024: £14,637m). Looking at the long-term debt bonds, the notes are at low coupon costs on the whole. The exceptions are the longer-dated instruments: a 6.375% sterling medium-term note maturing 2039 (£627m) and a 6.375% United States dollar medium-term note maturing 2038, which at £2.0bn of carrying value is a bit on the high side on its own. Elsewhere coupons run from 1.625% to around 5.375%, presumably because much of the book was taken out a while ago in a cheaper rate environment.

There is a danger here into the longer term. As the cheaper notes start rolling over into a higher interest rate environment, the company might see a slight rise in its long-term debt costs.

Valuation

In an overall sense I'm quite happy with the company and what it's been doing. Profitability came in very high last year at 172.0p of adjusted earnings per share, but I don't think we can assume earnings will continue at such a high growth rate, because I don't see a particular marker here to suggest sales can increase that dramatically. There's always a hope factor with pharmaceuticals that another miracle-type drug might explode revenue further, but I'm not sure GSK is on the cusp of this — maybe I'm wrong about that.

Bearing this in mind, I think it's only sensible to use a growth rate of about 6%, and that's what I'm using. I think GSK is quite a safe company and reasonably well protected in difficult markets, so I'm giving it an uplift of 5% on the valuation.

I've changed the valuation metric, and instead of the company's adjusted 172.0p I'm using a new earnings per share figure, taking reported profit before tax and assuming the following adjustments into the future:

🎓 Clarity Why value on 113.5p when the company reports 172.0p? Earnings per share (EPS) is the profit attributable to each ordinary share. GSK publishes two versions: a reported figure of 138.8p, which is what the statutory accounts say, and an adjusted or "core" figure of 172.0p, which strips out items management considers one-off — amortisation and impairment of acquired intangibles, restructuring, legal charges and the ViiV contingent consideration. My problem is that several of those items turn up every single year, so calling them one-off flatters the picture. So I have taken reported profit before tax and deducted the charges I think genuinely recur, giving 113.5p. That 113.5p is an override — my own forward assumption, not a figure GSK publishes — and it is what every number in the snapshot below is built on. It is also the whole thesis: on 172.0p the shares look like 10.6 times earnings; on my 113.5p they are 16.0 times.

Given the new earnings figure of 113.5p per share, the value opportunity at the current share price comes out at -22.4%. Ultimately I don't think there's a huge amount of risk with GSK — the company seems very stable, and I imagine it'll continue with slow increases in its profitability — but I don't like how much adjusting is happening to the income statement, and sales aren't progressing as fast as is warranted at the current share price. The upside potential isn't that wonderful at this stage.

🎓 Clarity How does the "value opportunity" work? My model takes the earnings per share, grows it by the growth rate over the multiple, adds it back to the starting earnings, then applies the multiple again: (113.50 × 0.06 × 8) + 113.50 = 167.98, and 167.98 × 8 = 1,343.84p. The 5% uplift for the company's safety takes that to 1,411.03p, my actual valuation. The value opportunity is simply that valuation divided by the market price, minus one: 1,411.03 ÷ 1,817.50 − 1 = -22.4%. A negative number means the market is asking more than my workings say the shares are worth. Multiplying the valuation by the shares in issue gives a valued market capitalisation of £56.5bn against £72.8bn today.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Sell case)

The honest bull case against my own verdict, in the order it worries me:

  • The hope factor: There's always a hope factor with pharmaceuticals that another miracle-type drug might explode revenue further. I'm not sure GSK is on the cusp of this — but maybe I'm wrong about that, and a single breakthrough would make a 6% growth rate look far too mean.
  • Specialty keeps compounding: The company's 2026 planning assumption is low double-digit percentage growth in Specialty Medicines. Oncology grew 43% to £2.0bn with Jemperli up 89% and Ojjaara up 60%, both still early in their launches. If that carries on, group growth is not stuck at 4%.
  • Shingrix is barely penetrated abroad: Markets outside the United States are now 66% of global Shingrix sales, up from 56%, and the average immunisation rate across the ten biggest of those is around 10%. Europe grew 42% after the French launch. That is a long runway if it converts.
  • The market may keep paying on the adjusted number: On the company's own 172.0p the shares are on about 10.6 times earnings, which does not look expensive at all. My case rests on 113.5p being the better figure; if investors keep valuing the adjusted basis, the price need never come to my valuation.
  • The business itself is not the risk: I don't think there's a huge amount of risk with GSK — the company seems very stable, and I imagine it'll continue with slow increases in its profitability. This is a price call, not a quality call, and price calls can stay wrong for a long time.
  • Debt costs are a slow drag, not a shock: As the cheaper notes roll over into a higher interest rate environment the company might see a slight rise in its long-term debt costs — a headwind to my earnings basis, but a gradual one.

What Would Change My Mind

  • Sales actually accelerating: Growth has been 3.4%, 3.5% and 4.1% over three years. A sustained step up well above that — not one good divisional quarter — would undermine my 6% growth rate assumption.
  • Fewer adjustments: If the gap between reported 138.8p and adjusted 172.0p narrows because the amortisation, impairment and ViiV contingent consideration charges genuinely stop recurring, my 113.5p override rises and the valuation rises with it.
  • A genuine miracle drug: Evidence that something in the pipeline — the antibody-drug conjugates, efimosfermin, bepirovirsen, the mRNA flu programme — is on the cusp of exploding revenue rather than merely progressing.
  • The dolutegravir cliff being covered: Long-acting injectables Cabenuva and Apretude are already 30% of United States HIV sales. If they grow into the whole of the exposure before loss of exclusivity bites, the largest risk under the biggest franchise shrinks.
  • The price coming to me: At or below 1,411.03p the value opportunity goes to zero or positive and this stops being a Sell on arithmetic alone.

Bottom Line — Sell

I'm quite happy with GSK and what it's been doing. It's a very large, very stable business, gross margin is high at 72.4%, the cost base outside research isn't growing, and Specialty Medicines is genuinely delivering. There isn't a huge amount of risk here.

But I don't like how much adjusting is happening to the income statement, and sales aren't progressing as fast as is warranted at the current share price. On my own 113.5p earnings figure, 6% growth, an 8x multiple and a 5% uplift for safety, the shares are worth 1,411.03p against 1,817.50p in the market. The upside potential isn't that wonderful at this stage.

Sell. Value opportunity: -22.4%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: A.
