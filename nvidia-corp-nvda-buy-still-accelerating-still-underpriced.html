Business Model

Nvidia started off as a company doing graphics chips for gaming personal computers, but it has morphed in recent years into something a bit different.

They design chips and platforms, while partners manufacture the wafers, package the chips and assemble much of the equipment. Nvidia are very much at the premium end and, at this current stage, are the leader in being able to design the best chips and platforms for artificial intelligence.

The company produces processors and chips, or helps produce them. Even when you have very good chips and the ability to compute, it's not as useful as it could be when it spends a lot of time communicating. Artificial intelligence clusters need to move data rapidly between processors, and that's where the networking part of their sales comes in.

Nvidia sells the connections within tightly coupled systems and the networks between systems, with hardware and supporting software.

For our analysis we will use their five end market divisions:

  • Data Center
  • Gaming
  • Professional Visualization
  • Automotive
  • OEM and Other — OEM meaning original equipment manufacturer, the companies that build finished machines

A physical product

Something worth highlighting is that this company still works in the physical. Their end product is physical.

They've seen incredible rises in revenue. Their design and implementation of chips may not be held back so much by them, but by their partners and their ability to supply huge amounts of chips.

I expect that demand will still go up exponentially into the future. You still have this situation where their growth has been so high just recently, and the question of how quickly everyone can ramp up their systems.

If you compare this to something like Google usage, there you set up a platform and almost any number of people can join that platform, with much less of an issue as to growth problems. Nvidia is not quite the same because they still act in the physical end product and not something that is in the cloud or hosted elsewhere.

Supply and capacity commitments were $95.2bn at the end of January 2026 and $279bn at the end of July, mainly for memory. Inventory rose from $21.4bn to $31.6bn over the same six months. On the second quarter call the company said it expects revenue to grow by approximately 70% in 2028, and described that as a supply-constrained outlook.

🎓 Clarity What are "supply and capacity commitments", and why do they jump from $95.2bn to $279bn? They are orders Nvidia has already placed, or capacity it has already booked, with its manufacturing and memory partners — money it is contractually on the hook for before a single finished rack is sold. A near-tripling in six months is the clearest signal of how much product the company is preparing to ship, which is why a supply-constrained outlook here means "we could sell more if we could build more", not "demand is fading". The flip side is that those commitments are a liability if demand ever does fade — which is exactly what the $4.5bn H20 charge was, an order book that stopped being sellable.

How quickly it has grown

To have grown so much in such a short period of time indicates that this isn't something that is impossible for other chip designers to do. I don't think anyone is on the cusp of doing that but it is always worth bearing in mind.

Many of the people that work in Nvidia now, to equal these kind of figures, weren't here five or six years ago. They have their act together, but to some extent it is at least conceivable that someone else could operate another business like it.

They've gone from a company with $16.7bn of revenue, a fair-sized company in the first place, to one with $215.9bn. The company had around 42,000 employees at the end of January 2026, around 31,000 of them in research and development.

Six Years of Results

Their growth in recent times has been astronomical. In 2024, the year to the end of January 2024, revenue grew by 125.9%. In 2025 it grew by 114.2% and in 2026 by 65.5%.

It is worth noting that these incredible rates of growth are coming down. In truth, that 65.5% last year was increasing their revenue by $85.4bn, a ridiculously high level. To put that into context, total revenue in 2024 was $60.9bn. Back in 2021, total revenue was $16.7bn.

Revenue and gross margin, 2021 to 2026

Source: company reported accounts, years to end of January. Bars are revenue, the gold line is gross margin.

Gross margins have been very robust. From 62.3% in 2021 they rose to 64.9% in 2022. In 2023 they did drop down to 56.9%. In 2024 they rebounded quite dramatically to 72.7%, in 2025 they saw 75.0% and in 2026 71.1%.

With this increase in revenue, their pre-tax profit return has generally increased proportionally. It did slip down in 2023, when gross margins were a bit weak.

In 2024, 2025 and 2026 it has been sitting very high. In 2026, 64.3% of turnover turned into pre-tax profit, their second highest proportional figure. That's an astounding level for a company that is most definitely trying to put a huge amount of effort into expansion and pushing forwards into the future.

On the reported accounts, pre-tax profit for 2026 was $141.5bn, 65.5% of turnover, which includes $8.9bn of gains on investments.

Correspondingly, earnings per share have risen very dramatically over the last years. In 2026 they grew by 66.7%. On a three-year average they grew by 204% a year, and even over a five-year average period they grew by 95.2% a year.

🎓 Clarity Why is the year ending in January 2026 called "2026", and what do these profit terms mean? Nvidia's financial year closes at the end of January, and the company labels each year by the calendar year it ends in — so "2026" is the twelve months to the end of January 2026, and the year currently running, "2027", ends in January 2027. On the profit lines: gross margin is what is left of each sales dollar after the direct cost of making the product; pre-tax profit (often shortened to PBT) is profit after all operating costs but before tax; post-tax profit is what remains after tax; and earnings per share (EPS) is that post-tax profit divided by the number of shares. EPS is the figure my valuation runs on, so it is the one to keep an eye on.

Why gross margin was weaker in 2023

Gross margin fell from 64.9% in 2022 to 56.9% in 2023. Revenue was flat at $27.0bn, with Gaming revenue down -27% to $9.07bn.

The company took $2.17bn of inventory provisions in 2023, against $354m the year before. Around $1.04bn was for inventory on hand and around $1.13bn was for purchase obligations in excess of its demand projections, mostly excess supply of Gaming and Data Center products.

The net effect of the provisions was to take 7.5 percentage points off the gross margin. Pre-tax profit in 2023 was also reduced by a $1.35bn write-off when the acquisition of Arm was terminated.

The dip to 71.1% in 2026 has a similar cause. A $4.5bn charge for H20 inventory and purchase obligations took the first quarter gross margin to 60.5%, and it was back at 75.0% by the fourth quarter.

🎓 Clarity What is an "inventory provision", and why does it dent the margin in one go? When a company holds stock it no longer expects to sell at full value — or has committed to buy parts it no longer needs — accounting rules make it write that value off immediately, as a charge against cost of sales. That single charge lands in one period's gross margin even though the problem built up over several. It is why 2023's 56.9% and 2026's 71.1% both understate the underlying run rate: strip the one-off out and 2026 ended the year back at 75.0% in the fourth quarter. That matters for this article because my valuation is built on margins holding around 75%, not on the 71.1% headline.

Clarity

These huge tech companies are very complex. Ultimately it still comes back to some of the same factors: how fast is revenue growing, how well are their margins doing, is it continuing, and is it growing at record paces?

The answer is yes.

Revenue grew 65.5% last year and has grown 85.2% and 105.9% in the first two quarters of this year. Gross margin has gone from 71.1% to around 75%.

It's undoubtable that there are huge levels of demand. Their margins, instead of drifting, are strengthening, and there seems to be no holding back for demand of their products and their services.

However, I am slightly concerned about their ability to continue to grow at quite this level of clip going forwards, although there's not very much to claim that they're not going to grow at this rate into the medium term.

The change at the top

Jensen Huang resigned as president, chief executive and a director on 12 November 2026, after 33 years. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer since 2013, is interim chief executive and Harvey Jones has become chair of the board.

My feelings are that the resignation of Jensen Huang is a sidebar. I don't think that is going to realistically derail the profitability of Nvidia in the coming years.

Valuation

I'm assuming an increase on average in the next two quarters of 90% in revenue for the company which, added to the existing quarters, means that I'm projecting a total revenue for the year of $290.45bn.

Because gross margins have improved, up from 71.1% last year to about 75% this year, I think that the company will be able to achieve $191.7bn of pre-tax profit.

At their current tax rate that would equate to a post-tax profit of $159.11bn, or 658.9 cents per share.

By my own projections the company is going to hit profit growth of 37.99% this year, so I'm using 40%, rounding up a little bit. Roughly that going into the future might be slightly optimistic, but at the same time they have seen dramatic increases in recent years.

I am going to give it a 50% probability because I think you can't argue too much with the growth factors that we have here.

There are lots of other factors that we could build into this valuation, US economic policy, Debt, Customer overconcentration to name just a few, but ultimately at this point in time, I think the most important points are, where are revenues heading, and what is happening with margins, both of these point to some strong profit figures in the coming year.

As a result, I come up with a valuation of 22,139.04 cents per share which, on the current share price of 16,285 cents, gives us a value opportunity of +35.9%.

So I'm giving it a buy on a research grade of A.

🎓 Clarity Why does the valuation use 658.90 cents of earnings when last year's reported figure was 490.0 cents? Because the model is being run on an override (FY2027e) — a hand-entered earnings per share figure for the year now in progress, rather than the audited number for the year just finished. The override of 658.90 cents comes straight from the projection above: $191.7bn of pre-tax profit, taxed at the current rate, giving $159.11bn of post-tax profit across 24,147m shares. It is a forecast, not a fact, and that is the single biggest judgement in this page: if that 658.90 cents is wrong, the 22,139.04 cent valuation moves with it. It also means the price-to-earnings ratio can be quoted two ways — 24.7 times on the override, 33.2 times on last year's reported 490.0 cents. Both are in the snapshot below so nothing is hidden.
🎓 Clarity How does the valuation formula actually work? It takes earnings per share, grows them by the growth rate, scales that growth by the multiple, adds the original earnings back, and applies the multiple again. Step by step here: 658.90 × 0.40 × 8 = 2,108.48; plus 658.90 = 2,767.38; × 8 = 22,139.04 cents. With no uplift or reduction applied, that is also the actual valuation. The value opportunity is then simply that valuation divided by the price: 22,139.04 ÷ 16,285 − 1 = +35.9%. The two levers that matter are the 40% growth rate and the multiple of 8 — a lower growth assumption or a lower multiple pulls the whole answer down quickly.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong

  • The rate of growth is coming down: 125.9%, then 114.2%, then 65.5%. I am slightly concerned about their ability to continue to grow at quite this level of clip going forwards. The valuation leans on 40% profit growth, so a sharper deceleration than that bites directly.
  • It is a physical product: their design and implementation of chips may not be held back so much by them, but by their partners and their ability to supply huge amounts of chips. The company itself called the approximately 70% growth expected in 2028 a supply-constrained outlook.
  • Commitments and inventory are building fast: supply and capacity commitments went from $95.2bn at the end of January 2026 to $279bn at the end of July, mainly for memory, with inventory up from $21.4bn to $31.6bn. The 2023 and 2026 charges show what happens when committed supply meets softer demand — $2.17bn of provisions in 2023, $4.5bn on H20 in 2026.
  • Customer overconcentration: two direct customers were 22% and 14% of revenue in 2026, and Data Center is 89.7% of the group.
  • China: revenue from customers headquartered in China and Hong Kong fell -21.4% to $19.7bn in 2026, and the third quarter 2027 outlook assumes no Data Center compute revenue from China at all.
  • Someone else could do this: to have grown so much in such a short period of time indicates that this isn't something that is impossible for other chip designers to do. I don't think anyone is on the cusp of doing that but it is always worth bearing in mind.
  • Factors I have not built into the valuation: US economic policy, debt and customer overconcentration among them. I left them out deliberately, but they are real.
  • The change at the top: Jensen Huang resigned on 12 November 2026 after 33 years. My feelings are that this is a sidebar — I don't think it is going to realistically derail the profitability of Nvidia in the coming years — but it is a change at the top of a company that has been run by one person for three decades.

What Would Change My Mind

  • Revenue stops running at this pace: my projection assumes an average 90% increase across the next two quarters. The third quarter guidance of $108.0bn is 89.5% growth — if the actual print and the following quarter come in materially under that, the $290.45bn revenue year and the 658.9 cent earnings figure go with it.
  • Margins start drifting instead of strengthening: gross margin was 74.9% and 75.0% in the first two quarters, with 74.0% guided for the third and 71% to 72% for the fourth. A slide below that guided range would undercut the $191.7bn pre-tax profit assumption.
  • The 2028 growth outlook gets cut: the company has said approximately 70%, supply-constrained. A reduction there would say the supply ramp, not demand, has become the binding problem for longer than I am assuming.
  • Fresh inventory or purchase-obligation charges: with commitments at $279bn, a charge on the scale of 2023's $2.17bn or 2026's $4.5bn would tell me the order book has got ahead of real demand.
  • Loss of a very large customer: with two customers at 22% and 14% of revenue, either stepping back would change the arithmetic rather than just the tone.

Bottom Line — Buy

It still comes back to the same two questions: where are revenues heading, and what is happening with margins. Revenue grew 65.5% last year and 85.2% and 105.9% in the first two quarters of this one. Gross margin has gone from 71.1% to around 75%. Both point to some strong profit figures in the coming year.

On $290.45bn of revenue, $191.7bn of pre-tax profit and 658.9 cents of earnings per share, growing at 40% on a multiple of 8, I get 22,139.04 cents against a share price of 16,285 cents. I'm slightly concerned about their ability to keep growing at quite this clip, and their end product is physical so the ramp depends on partners — which is why the probability is 50% and the risk factor 20%. But you can't argue too much with the growth factors that we have here.

Buy. Value opportunity: +35.9%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: A.
