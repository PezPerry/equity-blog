Full Pubs, Flat Earnings

Full-year results for the 52 weeks to 26 July 2026. All figures as reported in pounds sterling; per-share figures in pence.

Briefing

  • Thesis: I don't really share the market sentiment on this one at 915p. A well-run business with a property estate worth a good deal more than the balance sheet shows, but with earnings that will not escalate from here.
  • The numbers: Revenue up 5.2% to £2,238.0m with like-for-like sales up 4.2%, but operating profit before separately disclosed items down 17.9% to £120.2m and profit before tax before those items down 28.0% to £58.6m. Basic earnings per share 59.5p against 60.0p.
  • Key strength: Free cash flow nearly doubled to £100.1m, net debt excluding leases came down 1.2% to £715.8m, and 6.2% of the opening share count was bought back for £46.0m at an average of 656p.
  • Key risk: Costs. Repairs and maintenance up 32.3% to £132.0m, social security up 34.7% to £74.9m, and government policy that is not on their side on alcohol or on business rates.
  • Overall stance: Sell. My valuation is 647.36p against a 915p share price — a value opportunity of −29.3%, at a 40% risk factor and 50% probability, research grade A+.

Business Model

Wetherspoon runs 792 managed pubs in the United Kingdom and the Republic of Ireland, plus 23 franchised pubs, selling drink, food, slot machines and hotel rooms at low prices on high volumes.

Eight managed pubs opened in the year and there were 15 disposals — 10 trading pubs and five non-trading properties — which brought in £10.1m of cash. Fifteen franchised pubs opened, and the company plans around 15 managed pubs and 15 to 20 franchises in the current year.

Managed pub numbers moved from 794 to 792 over the year; the franchise estate went from 8 to 23. The company employs 24,200 full-time equivalents (FTEs — part-time hours converted into whole-time jobs), of which 19,095 are hourly-paid pub staff.

The freehold estate has been built up over the years by buying the freeholds of pubs where the company was the tenant: at the 2024 year end 72% of pubs were freehold, up from 41% in 2010.

The Year in Numbers

Revenue rose 5.2% to £2,238.0m, with like-for-like sales up 4.2%: bar sales up 6.1%, food up 1.2%, slot machines up 7.4% and hotel rooms up 1.3%.

Operating profit before separately disclosed items fell 17.9% to £120.2m, taking the operating margin down to 5.37% from 6.88%. The company puts the fall down to costs rising 5.3%, including £46m of wage increases, £31m of repairs and £9m of business rates.

Profit before tax (PBT — profit after all operating and interest costs but before the tax charge) and separately disclosed items fell 28.0% to £58.6m from £81.4m. After separately disclosed items, which were a £19.1m gain this year against a £7.9m gain last year, profit before tax was £77.7m against £89.3m.

Basic earnings per share (EPS — profit after tax divided by the average number of shares) were 59.5p including the separately disclosed items, against 60.0p last year. Without them, earnings per share were only 42.4p against 50.8p.

What Matters for This Company

Costs and the repairs line

Total employee costs increased 5.9% to £891.9m. Wages and salaries rose 4.0% to £787.0m while social security costs jumped 34.7% to £74.9m on the higher employer national insurance. I imagine this is generally not just an issue for the company. Higher wages and employer costs are a problem for most pubs and restaurants; even though they're a very efficient company, Wetherspoon are not immune to these rising costs.

Purchased food and drink, the inventory expense within cost of sales, was up 5.1% to £725.8m from £690.8m, which broadly matched sales growth. Employment charges ran roughly in line with the sales increase and food and drink basically ran with the increase in sales growth, which is maybe slightly disappointing.

Repairs and maintenance rose 32.3% to £132.0m from £99.8m, the biggest movement in the cost base. At the same time, reinvestment in existing pubs fell to £26.3m from £62.5m. This may be related to the changing mix in their asset estate. The two lines together come to £158.3m, against £162.2m last year and £190.9m the year before.

I am minded to feel that actually the repairs haven't increased, so we'll have to add this back within the figures. The gross margin squeeze is possibly not real either: gross profit fell to £179.2m from £200.3m with cost of sales up 6.8% against revenue up 5.2%, and the repairs line sits within cost of sales. It is just changes in how the accounts are being reworked.

🎓 Clarity Why does it matter whether estate spending is "repairs" or "reinvestment"? Repairs are expensed — they come straight off this year's profit. Reinvestment is capitalised — it sits on the balance sheet as an asset and only hits profit slowly, through depreciation. Shift the same pound from one bucket to the other and reported profit moves without the underlying business changing. That is the point above: combined spend on the estate actually fell (£162.2m to £158.3m), yet the expensed portion jumped 32.3%, which is why the margin squeeze is possibly not real.

Finance costs and net debt

Net finance costs before separately disclosed items were £61.9m against £64.0m, with bank interest down to £41.1m from £45.1m as the cost of debt fell to 6.11% from 6.57%. After the separately disclosed finance credits of £20.8m — a £13.0m fair value gain on the interest-rate swaps and £7.8m of hedge reserve amortisation — net finance charges reduced 24.8% to £41.1m from £54.6m.

Net debt excluding leases reduced 1.2%, not very much but a little bit, to £715.8m from £724.3m. Including lease liabilities, debt fell to £1.11bn from £1.12bn. Cash sits at £52.8m, a nice increase from £38.7m.

I am pleased that they're reducing their net debt. Since the year end a £98m private placement (a loan placed directly with institutional lenders rather than raised in public bond markets) matured and was repaid from existing facilities rather than renewed, and a new £500m interest-rate swap has been taken out for February 2030 to February 2032 at 4.45%.

Operating cash of £284.3m was largely absorbed by interest and tax, capital spending, lease payments and £87.8m of buybacks, dividends and employee share purchases.

Cash flow and capital investment

Total capital investment was £74.2m against £117.0m. £28.7m of this was invested in new pubs and pub extensions, £26.3m in existing pubs, £5.6m in business and IT projects and £13.6m in freehold reversions of properties where Wetherspoon was the tenant.

That's a reasonable level of reversion cost for the year, and it's down from £18.7m the previous year. Three reversions were completed. Still, £13.6m in freehold reversions in a year where there were 15 disposals seems a very high charge.

Free cash flow of £100.1m came after £31.9m of capital payments for existing pubs, business and IT projects (2025: £74.1m), £28.5m of share purchases for the employee share scheme and payments of tax and interest. Of that, £46.0m went on the buyback and £13.3m on dividends.

The company bought back 6,960,981 shares, 6.2% of the opening share count, for £46.0m at an average of 656p, and spent a further £28.5m on shares for the employee share scheme.

The property estate

The net book value of assets at the end of the period was £1.39bn, which the company says is 7.6 times the last twelve months' EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — a rough proxy for operating cash generation) of £181.2m. The freeholds have not been revalued since 1999. That's 27 years since they revalued their freehold property, and the figure in the balance sheet seems misleading as a result.

🎓 Clarity What is NAV, and why doesn't the property surplus show up in the valuation? NAV — net asset value, or net assets — is everything the company owns minus everything it owes: £336.8m here, which is about 319.5p a share on 105.41m shares. Because the freeholds haven't been revalued since 1999, that figure understates the estate; the rough guesstimate above would add some £875m, or roughly 1,149.6p a share in total. But the model is being run in earnings mode, not asset mode: assets per share is left blank and the valuation uplift is set at 0.00%, so none of that surplus is in the 647.36p. The surplus is why this is called a well-run business; the earnings are why it is called a Sell.

The separately disclosed items

I have to commend Wetherspoon, because they do like to rebase their accounts on what they see fit; one thing they do well is the income statement — they're providing greater clarity than they necessarily need to.

The chairman, Tim Martin, is very strident and has a lot of opinions. He does go on a bit about unfair tax treatment the company is subject to, which may be true, but I find it surprising that he's still going on about COVID and the treatment of the industry during lockdown. The tax system on pubs and bars is what it is, and I cannot see it changing anytime soon.

On the adjustments, property gains and losses were a loss of £2.1m in this period, as opposed to a gain of £2.7m in the previous year. That's fair enough, and for future valuation I'd figure this at zero, because some years it'll be up and some years it'll be down.

🎓 Clarity Two earnings per share figures — which one is the valuation using, and why? "Separately disclosed items" are one-off or non-trading gains and losses that the company reports apart from day-to-day trading: this year a £19.1m pre-tax gain, mostly the £20.8m of finance credits from the interest-rate swaps, less the £2.1m property loss. Include them and basic EPS is 59.5p; strip them out and underlying EPS is 42.4p. The valuation below uses 59.5p, entered as an override (override, FY2026e) — the reported basic figure, not the underlying one. That is the higher of the two, so the 647.36p valuation and the resulting −29.3% value opportunity are struck on the more generous earnings base, not the harsher one. For reference, 915p on the 42.4p pre-item figure would be 21.6 times earnings against the 15.4 times shown in the snapshot.

Clarity

I don't really share the market sentiment on this one at 915p. Despite being a great operator, I think they're going to struggle to see upward momentum in their earnings.

I've no doubt that greater implementation of AI and automated systems within their processes may improve results even more with time, but I do think they will struggle to improve EPS growth significantly.

Consumer sentiment is not particularly strong, and government policy is not on their side. It's not on their side as far as alcohol sales are concerned, and it's not on their side as far as business rates are concerned. It's also not looking like it's going to change any time soon.

My feelings are that this is a well-run business with a property estate worth a good deal more than the balance sheet shows, but with earnings that will not escalate from here.

Valuation

I'm using the basic earnings per share of 59.5p with a growth rate of 4.5%, which, if anything, could be seen as a little bit punchy. What is going to help the company on the upside in future years? Not a huge amount. What could affect it on the downside? Higher rates of inflation potentially increasing restriction in consumer spending, and potentially greater issues with government policy. As a result, I'm giving a risk factor of 40%.

My valuation comes in at 647.36p per share, which, given that the share price currently sits at 915p, gives it a −29.3% value opportunity.

The mechanics, for transparency: 59.50p of earnings grown at 4.5% over an 8.00x multiple gives 21.42p, added back to the 59.50p base for 80.92p, then put on the same 8.00x multiple — 647.36p. No uplift or reduction is applied, and assets per share is left blank, so the model runs on earnings.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Sell case)

The honest bull case against this verdict, from the same set of figures:

  • Current trading is strong: in the nine weeks to 27 September 2026 like-for-like sales rose 8.6%, which the company says was helped by exceptional weather, and it expects profit before tax and separately disclosed items for the current year in line with the company-compiled consensus of £74m — well above FY26's £58.6m.
  • The margin squeeze may not be real: if the repairs haven't increased and need adding back within the figures, reported profit understates the trading performance, and the gross margin fall is just changes in how the accounts are being reworked.
  • The property is worth more than the book: freeholds have not been revalued since 1999, and the rough guesstimate is an additional £875m above the £336.8m of net assets — none of which is in the 647.36p.
  • Cash and the balance sheet are improving: free cash flow nearly doubled to £100.1m, net debt excluding leases fell 1.2% to £715.8m, cash rose to £52.8m, the £98m private placement was repaid rather than renewed, and the cost of debt fell to 6.11% from 6.57%.
  • The share count is shrinking: 6,960,981 shares, 6.2% of the opening count, bought back for £46.0m at an average of 656p — well below today's 915p. Fewer shares means the same profit spread over a smaller base.
  • Technology could surprise: greater implementation of AI and automated systems within their processes may improve results even more with time.
  • The growth rate is a judgement, not a fact: 4.5% could be seen as a little bit punchy on my own reading — but an 8.00x multiple is also a choice, and a higher one would narrow the gap to 915p.

What Would Change My Mind

  • Evidence of real EPS escalation: the whole thesis is that earnings will not escalate from here. Profit before tax and separately disclosed items beating the £74m consensus, and doing it on trading rather than on swap credits, would undermine it.
  • The repairs line normalising: repairs and maintenance falling back from £132.0m while reinvestment in existing pubs recovers from £26.3m, confirming the add-back and a genuine margin that is better than 5.37%.
  • Food starting to work: food is 37% of revenue and grew only 1.7%. Bar sales did the work this year; food pulling its weight would change the earnings arithmetic.
  • Government policy turning: it's not on their side on alcohol sales and not on their side on business rates, and it's not looking like it's going to change any time soon. If it did, that is a direct earnings item.
  • Consumer sentiment improving: sentiment is not particularly strong and higher inflation could restrict consumer spending further — the main input behind the 40% risk factor.
  • A freehold revaluation: 27 years is a long time. A proper revaluation would put the property surplus into the accounts rather than into a guesstimate, and would be grounds to look at this on assets rather than on earnings.
  • The price coming to the valuation: 647.36p is where the earnings support the shares on these inputs. At that level this is a different conversation.

Bottom Line — Sell

Revenue up 5.2%, like-for-like sales up 4.2%, and operating profit before separately disclosed items down 17.9%. They sold more and earned less, and the earnings per share that holds up — 59.5p against 60.0p — only holds up because of the separately disclosed items; before them it is 42.4p against 50.8p.

This is a well-run business with a property estate worth a good deal more than the balance sheet shows, but with earnings that will not escalate from here. Consumer sentiment is not particularly strong and government policy is not on their side on alcohol or on business rates. I don't really share the market sentiment on this one at 915p.

On 59.5p of earnings, 4.5% growth and an 8.00x multiple with no uplift, the valuation is 647.36p — a valued capitalisation of £682.4m against a market capitalisation of £964.5m.

Sell. Value opportunity: −29.3%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: A+.
