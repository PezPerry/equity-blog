Slipping Sales, Shrinking Guidance

Briefing

  • Thesis: Across the board, the company is seeing weaknesses in its sales. They're not dramatic weaknesses, but I don't like the direction of travel, and the market price doesn't reflect it.
  • The numbers: Q3 group sales £2,909m, like-for-like −2.6%, comparable −1.8%, organic −1.1%. Full-year profit before tax and adjusting items guided to £700m–£750m, down from £700m–£800m in August and £750m–£850m in May. My valuation: 60.07p against a 94.94p share price.
  • Key strength: Sporting Goods and Outdoor is the only division growing, organic sales up +5.8% over nine months. Net cash before leases was £311m at January and should be around £525m–£585m by the end of January 2027 on the company's own cash guidance.
  • Key risk: Adjusting items were £223m in FY26, 26.2% of the headline profit figure and roughly double the £106m of two years ago. Complementary Athleisure — the US fascias plus Courir — is down −4.6% organically over nine months.
  • Overall stance: Sell. 1% growth rate, 8× multiple, −15% valuation reduction, giving 60.07p and a −36.7% value opportunity. Research grade A, probability 50%, risk factor 20%.

Business Model

JD is a multi-brand retailer of sports, fashion and outdoor products, with 4,681 stores across 31 countries. Its fascias — the trading brands above the shop doors — include JD, Size?, Courir, DTLR, Shoe Palace, Hibbett, Sprinter, Sport Zone, Cosmos and Go Outdoors.

It reports through three divisions:

  • JD — the core fascia, 62% of group sales over the nine months.
  • Complementary Athleisure — Finish Line, Hibbett, DTLR, Shoe Palace and Courir.
  • Sporting Goods and Outdoor — Sprinter, Sport Zone and Go Outdoors.

Group sales for the quarter were £2,909m, and £8,808m for the nine months. North America is the largest region at 37% of third quarter sales, followed by Europe at 34%, the UK at 24% and Asia Pacific at 5%.

Third Quarter Trading

JD Sports have reported their third quarter trading update covering the 13 weeks to 31 October 2026.

Sales in apparel and accessories have been resilient, despite unseasonably warm weather delaying autumn and winter demand. Footwear remained soft. Store footfall was lower year on year, partly offset by better conversion in the stores. Online sales growth continued, up +0.8%, and this was particularly the case in North America.

In the UK, sales were softer against a second quarter that was boosted by the World Cup, with comparable sales at −1.3% against +2.2% in the second quarter. Europe was weak too, in line with its second quarter trend, with comparable sales at −2.3% against −2.1%. Trading there in October was also affected by civil unrest, especially in France. Gross margin for the quarter was within the company's expectations.

Like-for-like sales are weak across the company in the third quarter. North America saw like-for-like sales slip by −2.2%, Europe by −2.7%, the UK by −2.6% and Asia Pacific by −5.3%.

The group trend was a little better than the second quarter, when like-for-like sales were −3.1%, comparable sales −2.2% and organic sales −1.3%. North America improved the most, with comparable sales at −1.3% against −6.0% in the second quarter. Part of that was back-to-school demand moving from July into August.

🎓 Clarity Like-for-like, comparable, organic — what's the difference, and why are there three of them? They are three different ways of stripping out the noise from a sales number. Like-for-like (LFL) counts only stores and websites that were trading in both periods, so it tells you whether the existing estate is selling more or less. Comparable is a slightly wider measure that also picks up things like relocated or refitted space. Organic excludes businesses bought or sold during the period, so it shows growth without the help of acquisitions. All three here are at constant exchange rates, meaning last year's figures are restated at this year's currency rates so a weaker or stronger pound doesn't flatter or flatten the comparison. It matters for this company because the three measures are telling the same story in Q3 — −2.6%, −1.8% and −1.1% — so the softness isn't a currency effect or an accounting artefact. It's the shops.

Sporting Goods and Outdoor, the Sprinter, Sport Zone and Go Outdoors businesses, is the only division growing, with organic sales up +5.8% for the nine months. It is the smallest, at 13% of group sales.

🎓 Clarity What are "adjusting items", and why not just ignore them like the company does? Adjusting items are costs a company separates out of its headline profit because it considers them one-off or non-operational — writedowns of assets (impairment), the gradual writing-off of brand and customer lists bought in an acquisition (amortisation of acquired intangibles), restructuring, legal provisions. The headline number is "profit before tax and adjusting items"; the statutory number is what's actually left after them. Here the two have been pulling apart: £106m of adjusting items in FY24, £223m in FY26, which is 26.2% of the headline figure. When £188m of that is impairment and amortisation that turns up every single year, it is hard to treat it as exceptional. That is exactly why the valuation below starts from the guided headline profit and then deducts an allowance for adjusting items rather than valuing the headline number.

🎓 Clarity If the company has net cash, why does it also have £2.8bn of net debt? Both are true, and the difference is leases. Accounting rules make a retailer put the present value of all its future shop rents on the balance sheet as a liability — here £3,138m. Strip those out and JD actually holds more cash than bank borrowing: £311m of net cash before leases at January. Include them and you get £2,827m of net debt including leases. Neither figure is wrong; the leases are real obligations on 4,681 stores, but they are rent you pay as you trade rather than a loan that can be called in. Free cash flow — the cash left after running and investing in the business — is quoted here after those lease payments, which is why the cash pile still builds while the lease liability sits there.

What Matters for This Company

Across the board, the company is seeing weaknesses in its sales.

They're not dramatic weaknesses, but I don't like the direction of travel for the company right now. It seems to be affected by what I would deem a weak consumer sentiment period.

The guidance is slightly lower, but we are now past the third quarter, so the company has greater clarity on its margins and profits.

Valuation

Looking at and comparing past adjusting items, I'm going to use the middle ground figure of £725m as profit before tax and adjusting items for the full year.

Off that, I'm taking £215m as adjusting items, which gives me a pre-tax profit of £510m and a post-tax profit of £382.5m. This equates to earnings per share of 8.18p.

Whereas I previously gave a 3% growth figure for JD Sports, I'm retracting that down to a 1% growth rate, which is probably generous given where profitability seems to be heading this year and slipping sales in a general sense.

I'm also giving it a valuation reduction of −15% because I don't like where the company seems to be heading.

It has relied on acquisitions for growth in past years, and now, due to the wider economic environment, it is struggling with its like-for-like sales. I'm not too sure there's a huge amount the company can do about it.

My valuation comes in at 60.07p per share, which, given that the share price currently sits at 94.94p, gives a −36.7% value opportunity.

🎓 Clarity Why is the earnings per share here 8.18p when the company will report something much higher? Earnings per share (EPS) is profit after tax divided by the number of shares. This 8.18p is an override (FY2027e) — it isn't a reported figure, it's my own forecast, and it is deliberately built on the statutory route, not the headline one. I start from the company's guidance midpoint of £725m profit before tax and adjusting items, deduct £215m for adjusting items, tax the remaining £510m to get £382.5m, and divide by 4,673.49m shares. JD's own adjusted EPS starts from the £725m instead and would be roughly a third higher. The whole valuation turns on that choice: when impairment and amortisation of acquired intangibles alone have been running at £188m a year, I'd rather value the profit that survives them. The valuation snapshot below uses the 8.18p consistently, which is why the price-to-earnings ratio shows as 11.6× rather than the single digits an adjusted EPS would give you.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Sell case)

The bull case, set against my own:

  • The trend is already turning: the group was better in Q3 than Q2 on all three measures — like-for-like −2.6% against −3.1%, comparable −1.8% against −2.2%, organic −1.1% against −1.3%. North America improved most, comparable sales at −1.3% against −6.0%.
  • North America ex-Finish Line is growing: comparable sales there were +0.7% excluding Finish Line. With 126 standalone stores left to convert to JD, that drag has a finite life.
  • The weather, not the consumer: apparel and accessories were resilient and gross margin was within expectations; unseasonably warm weather delayed autumn and winter demand, which could simply land in the fourth quarter.
  • One division is working: Sporting Goods and Outdoor is up +5.8% organically over nine months and accelerating, +6.2% in Q3.
  • My adjusting-items allowance may be too harsh: first-half adjusting items were £41m against £213m a year earlier. If the full year lands nearer that run-rate than my £215m, the statutory profit and the EPS I'm valuing both go up materially.
  • The cash is real: free cash flow guidance of £460m–£520m is unchanged through all three updates, the £200m buyback is done, and net cash before leases should build to roughly £525m–£585m by January.
  • My inputs are deliberately low: an 8× multiple with a further −15% reduction is a hard mark-down. A 1% growth rate is close to assuming nothing improves at all.

What Would Change My Mind

  • Like-for-likes turning positive: group like-for-like sales back above zero, rather than merely less negative, would break the "direction of travel" objection that drives the −15% reduction.
  • Adjusting items shrinking: a full-year figure well below my £215m allowance — and impairment and amortisation of acquired intangibles falling rather than repeating — would lift the earnings I'm valuing.
  • Guidance holding: the top of the range has come down twice already, from £850m to £800m to £750m. A fourth-quarter outcome at or above £750m, with no further cut, would argue for restoring the growth rate.
  • Complementary Athleisure stabilising: organic sales back to flat, with the Finish Line conversions finished and US promotional intensity normalising.
  • The price doing the work: at 60.07p the value opportunity closes to zero. Below that, the arithmetic stops saying sell.

Bottom Line — Sell

Across the board, the company is seeing weaknesses in its sales. They're not dramatic weaknesses, but I don't like the direction of travel, and I'm not too sure there's a huge amount the company can do about it while the consumer is where it is.

On the £725m midpoint, £215m of adjusting items and 8.18p of earnings per share, a 1% growth rate and an 8× multiple give 70.68p. The −15% reduction takes that to 60.07p against a 94.94p share price.

Sell. Value opportunity: −36.7%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: A.
