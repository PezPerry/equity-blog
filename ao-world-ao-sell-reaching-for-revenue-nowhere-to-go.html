Reaching for Revenue, Nowhere to Go

Interim results for the six months to 30 September 2026, against financial years ending 31 March. All figures in sterling; per-share figures in pence.

Briefing

  • Thesis: A profitable, debt-free business that is reaching for revenues however it can. Consumer retail — seven tenths of the group — grew 3.1%, about where inflation may have taken things, and I don't go along with their plans for how they're going to increase sales.
  • The numbers: Revenue up 5.5% to £617.5m, operating profit up 18% to £20.6m, profit before tax up 20% to £21.5m. Full-year 2027 guidance is £48m to £54m of profit before tax, against the roughly £54.4m the company was previously guiding to, which is now the top of the range.
  • Key strength: The size of the white goods offering, delivered and installed through their own logistics operation, which is difficult for others to compete with. Borrowings of £1.6m and £93.9m of cash in hand.
  • Key risk: At a basic level their marketplace just isn't strong enough against the Amazons and general wider internet retailing of today, and the company is still lacking as to what they're going to do next.
  • Overall stance: Sell. 6.33p of earnings per share at 3.5% growth and a 7x multiple gives 55.17p against the 86.00p share price — a −35.9% value opportunity. Research grade A, probability 50%.

Business Model

AO World is an online electricals retailer built around a large white goods offering — fridges, washing machines, ovens and the like — which it delivers and installs through its own logistics operation. That last part matters: the vans, the drivers and the installers are theirs, not a courier's.

It reports six divisions:

  • Consumer retail: sales to households through its own websites.
  • Business-to-business retail: selling the same sort of product to companies rather than households.
  • Mobile: including AO Mobile, the company's own mobile network.
  • Recommerce: the second-hand technology business built on musicMagpie — buying used devices, refurbishing them and selling them on.
  • Third-party logistics: using the delivery and installation network to carry other companies' goods for a fee.
  • Recycling: processing old appliances and devices.

The First Half

AO World have reported their interim results for the six months ending 30 September 2026. Total revenues went up 6% to £617.5m. Impressively, operating profit increased by 18% to £20.6m. They hit a profit before tax for the half of £21.5m, 20% higher than the previous year.

On the outlook, the company has commented that it is mindful of market uncertainty and that trading conditions are challenging. They have expectations for the full year 2027 of profit before tax between £48m and £54m. Before these results the company was guiding to market expectations of around £54.4m, which is now the top of the range.

🎓 Clarity What is “profit before tax”, and why does guidance moving matter so much? Profit before tax (PBT) is what's left of sales after every cost of running the business — goods, wages, warehouses, advertising, depreciation and interest — but before the tax bill. It is the figure UK companies are usually judged on. “Guidance” is the company telling the market what it expects that figure to be. Here the single number the market was working to, roughly £54.4m, has become the top of a £48m to £54m range. Nothing has technically been downgraded, but the realistic centre of expectations has moved down — and that is the number a valuation gets built on.

🎓 Clarity What is “gross margin”, and why is a rising one not automatically good news? Gross margin is the share of each pound of sales left after the direct cost of the goods sold — so a 25.4% gross margin means 25.4p of every pound is available to cover warehouses, vans, advertising and head office. AO's gross margin has climbed every year from 19.3% to 25.4%, which is genuine progress on the quality of each sale. But margin per sale rising while total turnover sits below where it was in 2022 tells you the improvement is coming from how they sell, not from selling to more people. That distinction is the whole argument here.

The mobile side of the business has had a bit of an increase in turnover, but it's still quite small in a relative sense. It moved up to £37.5m in the first half, a 13.8% increase. Mobile made a profit before tax of £1.8m in the half, against a loss of −£1.0m a year earlier.

Administrative expenses rose 4.7% to £136.5m, against revenue growth of 5.5%.

  • Advertising and marketing went up £2.9m. Less traffic arrived through organic search — visitors who find the site free via Google rather than through paid adverts — so more of it had to be paid for.
  • Warehousing went up £2.7m on more warehouse space, higher volumes and higher labour rates.
  • Other administration, which is staff and office costs, was broadly flat. The new warehouse management system cost £2.0m more than a year earlier, offset by savings from moving roles offshore.

The company puts the inflation absorbed in the half at £2.1m or more, across rent reviews, business rates, staff and agency rates and the pay award.

The second half carries a further step-up of around £5m in technology and people, mostly the warehouse management system and artificial intelligence, plus around £2.5m more depreciation on new space and vehicles. Depreciation is the accounting charge that spreads the cost of capital expenditure — capex, the money spent on long-lived assets such as buildings and vans — across the years of its use.

Balance sheet

Overall, on their balance sheet, there wasn't very much to report. Just minor changes in the greater scheme of things.

  • No meaningful debt: one thing that is very positive for the company is that they don't have any big long-term borrowings. Borrowings are £1.6m.
  • Cash: they've got a little bit of cash in hand at £93.9m, which is some flexibility, but not a whole lot.
  • Undrawn facility: the £120m revolving credit facility is undrawn — an agreed overdraft-style line with the banks that can be dipped into and repaid, currently untouched.

🎓 Clarity Reported or adjusted — which earnings number is this valuation using, and why neither? “Reported” profit is the full statutory figure. “Adjusted” strips out items management considers one-off, such as the −£3.3m of musicMagpie deal costs and the −£19.6m impairment (a write-down in the carrying value of the mobile business) that together cut FY2025 from £43.5m adjusted to £20.6m reported. For FY2026 the two are identical at £50.5m, so there is no gap to argue about. This valuation uses neither historic figure: it uses the company's own forward guidance for FY2027, at the bottom of the range, because no adjusting items are expected this year. That is why the earnings per share in the snapshot below is flagged (override, FY2027e) rather than taken from last year's accounts.

What Matters for This Company

New ventures

  • The company launched AO Mobile earlier this year, which is their own mobile network.
  • They now, via a partnership with Timpson, cover more than 1,300 stores for device trade-ins, which increases their supply of refurbished phones.
  • They also acquired Jessops, a photography retailer. The price was £3.8m in cash on a debt free, cash free basis, for a business with revenue of around £20m, a pre-tax loss of around −£1m, 7 stores and an online operation.
  • In the next twelve months, AO is going to start launching solar, with electric vehicle charging and air conditioning to follow.
  • They're also going to spend £2m on testing robotics at scale in their operations, believing that physical artificial intelligence, including humanoid robots in the home, will become a significant consumer market.

That's all fine, but I'm not too sure how much the Jessops brand name is worth nowadays. It's been a long time since they seemed to be very relevant to the market.

In itself, I don't quite disagree with some of these things.

What I actually see

In an overall sense, I see the company just reaching for revenues however they can, and still unsure as to how to increase their offering.

The headline is that they're struggling to increase their consumer retail revenues, which is the all important side.

It's great that they've set up their mobile side of things. Mobile has made a profit before tax of £1.8m in the half, which is good news and encouraging.

Recommerce does seem to be gaining some revenues again. How much is this truly a long-term growth area for the company? I'm not seeing it.

It's underwhelming in my mind, and they've been playing around with all of these different areas for quite a while.

They're still struggling, and I don't go along with their plans for how they're going to increase sales. I think it's desperation rather than a good plan, selling the mobile networks and the second-hand devices.

At a basic level their marketplace just isn't strong enough against the Amazons and general wider internet retailing of today.

I would say they've been saved very much by the size of their white goods offering, which is difficult for others to compete with.

Where does the company end up going? I think they're going to see marginal profit improvements in their existing businesses, and these other areas may also bring in a little bit extra, but I don't think it's going to be transformational in any meaningful way.

The company is still lacking as to what they're going to do next, and there aren't very many easy options.

The one thing that does come to mind with AO World is merging with another existing operation, such as Currys, or maybe even another retailer, to provide a collaboration of different market areas together.

At their current valuations they are potentially affordable as an acquisition or as part of a merger. If ever so slightly on the expensive side, they are within reasonable grounds to be acquired or to merge with another operation.

I haven't heard any news that that is the direction in which they're going. I wouldn't value them on that basis, because who knows when, how and if something like that could happen.

I'm basing my valuation purely on what I'm seeing in front of me. What I can see is a profitable business, at least, that doesn't have debts, and that is quite positive, but with nowhere to go as an operation.

Valuation

I'm going to use the lower end of the guidance, at £48m of profit before tax.

The company expects an effective tax rate for the year of 27.4%, which gives a profit after tax of £34.85m.

On the shares in issue, the equivalent earnings per share is 6.33p.

I'm using this earnings per share of 6.33p, but only a growth rate of 3.5%. Recent averages of the company's earnings per share growth have been significantly higher than this, but I think they're reaching for turnover by any means, a practice they've been following for years.

This gives a valuation of 55.17p, a −35.9% value opportunity against the 86.00p share price.

🎓 Clarity How does this valuation actually work? Earnings per share (EPS) is the company's post-tax profit divided by the number of shares — here £34.85m ÷ 549.96m shares = 6.33p of profit behind each share. The model then grows that earnings figure at an assumed rate, capitalises it at a chosen multiple, and compares the result with the market price. In full: ((6.33 × 3.5% × 7) + 6.33) × 7 = 55.17p. With no uplift or reduction applied, that stands as the actual valuation. The value opportunity is simply how far the price would have to move to reach it: 55.17 ÷ 86.00 − 1 = −35.9%. A negative number means the shares are above what the earnings justify on these assumptions. The two levers that matter are the 3.5% growth rate and the 7x multiple — both deliberately modest, because the view is marginal profit improvement rather than transformation.

🎓 Clarity Why is the earnings figure an “override”, and what does the risk factor mean? An override means the earnings per share in the model was set by hand rather than lifted from the last published accounts. Here it is built from the company's own FY2027 guidance at its lower end: £48m of profit before tax, less the guided 27.4% effective tax rate, giving £34.85m of post-tax profit and 6.33p per share. Taking the bottom of the range is a deliberate choice, not an accounting fact — the top of the range would produce a higher figure. The risk factor of 20% is the assessed chance of something going materially wrong with the business case, and the probability of 50% is the confidence attached to this valuation being the right read.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Sell case)

These are the ways the Sell could be wrong — the bull case, stated firmly.

  • Guidance lands at the top, not the bottom: the valuation uses £48m of profit before tax. The range runs to £54m, and the roughly £54.4m the company was previously guiding to is now the top of that range. At the upper end, earnings per share and therefore the valuation are materially higher than 55.17p.
  • Growth assumption is deliberately low: recent averages of the company's earnings per share growth have been significantly higher than the 3.5% used here. If earnings growth reverts to those averages, the 7x multiple applied to a low growth rate understates the business.
  • Margin is still climbing: gross margin has risen every single year from 19.3% in 2022 to 25.4% in this half, and profit on turnover from 0.2% to 3.5%. That trend has not broken.
  • New divisions are starting to pay: mobile swung from a −£1.0m loss to a £1.8m profit before tax in the half, and recommerce grew 17.3%. If these keep compounding, the “little bit extra” becomes more than marginal.
  • Balance sheet gives them time: borrowings of £1.6m, £93.9m of cash and an undrawn £120m facility mean there is no financing pressure forcing anything.
  • Corporate action: at their current valuations they are potentially affordable as an acquisition or as part of a merger, with Currys or another retailer. I wouldn't value them on that basis — who knows when, how and if something like that could happen — but it is the obvious route by which a seller gets caught out.
  • The white goods moat: they've been saved very much by the size of their white goods offering, which is difficult for others to compete with. That protection does not disappear.

What Would Change My Mind

  • Consumer retail accelerating: growth in consumer retail moving clearly above the 3.1% recorded here, so it is doing more than keeping up with where inflation may have taken things. This is the all important side.
  • Full-year profit before tax at the top of the range: delivery at or near £54m, rather than the £48m I've used, with the second-half cost step-up of around £5m plus £2.5m more depreciation absorbed.
  • A plan I can go along with: evidence that solar, electric vehicle charging, air conditioning, mobile or recommerce is transformational rather than marginal — not more new areas, but one of them becoming significant.
  • Recommerce proving itself long-term: sustained growth that shows it is genuinely a long-term growth area and not a run of easy comparatives.
  • A confirmed merger or acquisition: actual news of a combination such as Currys. I haven't heard any, and I wouldn't value them on that basis — but it would change the arithmetic entirely.
  • Price doing the work: the shares moving towards 55.17p, which removes the −35.9% gap without the business needing to change at all.

Bottom Line — Sell

AO World is a profitable business that doesn't have debts, and that is quite positive, but with nowhere to go as an operation. Revenue up 5.5%, operating profit up 18% and profit before tax up 20% in the half is a decent set of numbers — and yet consumer retail, seven tenths of the group, managed 3.1%, about where inflation may have taken things.

I see the company just reaching for revenues however they can, and still unsure as to how to increase their offering. I don't go along with their plans for how they're going to increase sales; I think it's desperation rather than a good plan. Marginal profit improvements, yes. Transformational in any meaningful way, no.

On the lower end of guidance — £48m of profit before tax, taxed at 27.4% — earnings per share is 6.33p. At 3.5% growth and a 7x multiple that is 55.17p against a share price of 86.00p.

Sell. Value opportunity: −35.9%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: A.
