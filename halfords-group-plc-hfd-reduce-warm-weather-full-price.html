Warm Weather, Full Price

Interim results for the 26 weeks to 2 October 2026, set against my own estimate for the full year to April 2027. All figures in sterling; per-share figures in pence unless stated.

Briefing

  • Thesis: Pretty good, from a company that in recent years seems to have been struggling to move forward — but around £8m of the first-half profit was warm weather, and at 244.00p the share price already assumes the improvement carries on. I have a certain lack of confidence that it will.
  • The numbers: Group revenue £953.3m, up +6.7%. Gross margin 52.6% against 51.4%. Group underlying operating profit £43.9m, up £15.2m. Underlying profit before tax £39.0m, up +66.0%. Full-year guidance raised to £60m–£65m from £55m–£65m.
  • Key strength: Autocentres. The underlying operating margin went from 2.2% to 3.6% and underlying operating profit from £8.1m to £13.8m, and that division is now 41% of group sales.
  • Key risk: The weather. Management put about £6m of the Retail benefit and about £2m of the Autocentres benefit down to unusually warm conditions, and the company itself says first-half like-for-like growth and gross margin will not continue into the second half.
  • Overall stance: Reduce. On £62m of guidance less £2.5m of supply chain costs I get 20.6p of earnings, 2% growth, an 8× multiple and a valuation of 191.17p — a value opportunity of -21.7% against 244.00p. Research grade A, probability 50%, risk factor 20%.

Business Model

Halfords sells motoring and cycling products and services in the UK through two divisions.

  • Retail, split into Motoring and Cycling, with 366 Halfords stores and 2 Tredz stores.
  • Autocentres, with 497 consumer garages and 83 commercial fleet locations.

Retail is around 60% of group sales and Autocentres around 40%. Cut the other way, Motoring across both divisions is around 80% of group sales, with cycling the remaining 20%.

🎓 Clarity What is the difference between “underlying” and “reported” profit before tax? Profit before tax (PBT) is what is left after all costs but before the tax bill. “Reported” PBT is the statutory number — everything included. “Underlying” PBT strips out items the company treats as one-offs, here a net -£3.5m. That is why underlying PBT is £39.0m and reported PBT is £35.5m for the same six months. It matters for this company because the one-offs keep arriving: the valuation below deliberately takes £2.5m back off guidance on the view that they will carry on coming through, just at a lower level.

🎓 Clarity What is “like-for-like” growth, and why is it higher than the headline? Like-for-like (LFL) sales strip out the effect of opening and closing sites, and of a longer or shorter trading year, so you are comparing the same shops and garages against the same period last year. Group headline growth for the year to March 2026 was +2.9% but like-for-like was +4.8%, and Autocentres was +2.1% headline against +5.8% like-for-like — the gap is mostly site closures and the 52-week presentation. For the thesis here, LFL is the honest measure of trading, and it is the measure the company has told us will slow in the second half.

Cycling moved the most last year, up +6.3%. Motoring and Autocentres have risen each year at a slower rate.

Motoring didn’t shoot up last year; it went up only +1.8%.

Cycling, as the company are indicating, was up +6.3%. No doubt this is what they’re talking about when they talk about significant improvements because of good weather.

Cycling did very well in the COVID periods. Since then, I believe they have struggled in this particular department.

Their Autocentres are doing quite nicely as well, slowly drifting up, +2.1% last year, and they now take up a significant amount of total revenues at 41%.

Net Debt

Net debt also decreased slightly, down to £207.0m from £232.7m, including leases.

Not that dramatic, but I like the direction it is taking.

The company had net cash of £40.1m, against £18.6m a year earlier.

🎓 Clarity How can net debt be £207.0m and net cash be £40.1m at the same time? Because they measure different things. “Net cash” here is cash in the bank less borrowings — a positive £40.1m. “Net debt including leases” adds in the capitalised value of the rent owed on 366 stores and 580 garages and fleet sites, which is why it is a £207.0m liability. For a leasehold retailer both are worth watching: the first tells you about near-term flexibility, the second about the fixed obligations the business carries whatever the weather does.

Supply Chain Optimisation

Non-underlying items for the half were -£3.5m before tax, of which -£4.6m was supply chain optimisation, partly offset by £2.9m of compensation for compulsory store closures.

The supply chain costs relate to a new single national distribution centre, which the company expects to complete in its 2030 financial year.

The company expects £35m to £45m of non-underlying operating costs on this over three years, with capital expenditure of £40m to £50m, and an annual benefit in the low to mid teens of millions once it is mature.

🎓 Clarity What is capital expenditure, and why is it listed separately from the costs? Capital expenditure (capex) is money spent on long-lived assets — here the building, racking and systems of the new distribution centre — which is spread across the profit and loss account over years rather than charged all at once. The £35m to £45m of non-underlying operating costs are the disruption, dual-running and exit charges, which hit profit as they happen. That split is the point of the valuation adjustment below: the capex is a cash and balance-sheet question, but the operating costs are the ones that keep landing on reported profit until the project is finished in financial 2030.

What Matters for This Company

I do think that the company are starting to get their act together a bit more. Things do seem to be going in the right direction.

Despite my negativity towards companies that put weather down, either positively or negatively, as changing profitability, I suspect the company is going to be right in their claims about this.

I think they’re going to struggle to greatly improve profitability in the coming twelve months unless it happens to be warm, which is not something that I want to have a view on.

I have a certain lack of confidence that we’re going to see significant uplifts in their profitability from this year’s type of figures into the medium term.

Valuation

I’m going to take a middle part of their guidance and assume £62m of underlying profit before tax.

From this I am going to reduce it by -£2.5m for supply chain optimisation.

That’s because I think it will continue, just not at as high a level as it has been. The company have been going through a lot of restructuring to get themselves in line.

They’ve got their new distribution centre that they are working on, but they’re still three years away from completing that. I think there’s going to continue to be some items that will keep on coming through.

This gives me profitability for this year of £59.5m pre-tax, or £44.63m post-tax, which equates to 20.6p earnings per share.

🎓 Clarity Why does the snapshot show earnings per share as an “override”? Earnings per share (EPS) is profit after tax divided by the number of shares. The snapshot does not use the company’s published figure — it uses my own forward estimate of 20.6p for the year to April 2027, built from £62m of guidance less £2.5m of supply chain costs, taxed at 25% over 216.58m shares. That is what the model is told to value, so it is labelled (override, FY2027e). It is a deliberate divergence from the reported basis: the company’s first-half underlying EPS was 13.9p and its first-half statutory profit before tax was lower still at £35.5m. Every ratio on this page — the price-to-earnings ratio, the formula valuation, the value opportunity — is computed on the 20.6p override, not on the published numbers.
🎓 Clarity How does the model turn 20.6p into 191.17p? It grows the earnings by the growth factor over the multiple and then capitalises the result: ((EPS × growth × multiple) + EPS) × multiple. With 20.6p of earnings, 2% growth and an 8× multiple that is ((20.6 × 0.02 × 8) + 20.6) × 8 = 191.17p, with no uplift or reduction applied. The value opportunity is simply that valuation divided by the current price, minus one: 191.17 ÷ 244.00 − 1 = -21.7%. A negative number means the model says the price is above the value — which is the whole of the case here.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Reduce case)

The honest bull case against this stance is that the recovery is real and I am discounting it too hard. On the note’s own figures, that case runs as follows.

  • Another warm year: the company’s own £8m weather benefit cuts both ways. If next year is warm too, the comparative is met rather than missed, and the £60m to £65m guidance becomes a floor rather than a ceiling. I have explicitly declined to take a view on the weather, which means I could be wrong in either direction.
  • Autocentres has further to run: the underlying operating margin moved from 2.2% to 3.6% against a medium-term target of 5% to 6%. The company attributes part of that to better technician utilisation and warranty sales, neither of which is weather. On £725.2m of annual Autocentres sales, each further point of margin is material against £59.5m of estimated pre-tax profit.
  • Retail margin is already inside the target band: the Retail underlying operating margin was 5.3% in the half against 3.9% a year earlier, which is within the 5% to 6% medium-term target. If that holds without warm weather, my 2% growth assumption is too low.
  • The distribution centre is a real prize: the single national distribution centre is expected to deliver an annual benefit in the low to mid teens of millions once mature. Against £59.5m of estimated pre-tax profit that is a large number, and my -£2.5m adjustment charges the cost while giving no credit for the benefit.
  • Guidance has already been raised once: the range moved from £55m–£65m to £60m–£65m. I have taken the middle at £62m. A second upgrade, or a finish at the top of the range, would lift the earnings base the valuation rests on.
  • Debt is moving the right way: net debt including leases fell from £232.7m to £207.0m and net cash rose from £18.6m to £40.1m. A company with falling obligations and a 3.70% dividend yield can sustain a higher multiple than the 8× I have applied.
  • Cycling could keep going: cycling was up +6.3% in the year to March 2026 and +7.7% in the half, having struggled since the COVID periods. If that is a genuine turn rather than good weather, the 20% of group sales it represents stops being a drag.

What Would Change My Mind

  • Second-half trading that does not fade: the company has told us first-half like-for-like growth and gross margin will not continue, and that October and November have returned to more typical rates. Like-for-like growth and a 52.6%-type gross margin holding through the second half would contradict the central assumption.
  • Margin progress without the weather: Autocentres moving towards the 5% to 6% target in a period the company does not attribute to warm conditions. That would make better technician utilisation and warranty sales the driver rather than the sunshine.
  • The non-underlying items actually stopping: my -£2.5m adjustment assumes items keep coming through until the distribution centre completes in financial 2030. A clean run of periods with no material supply chain charge would remove it and lift the earnings base.
  • Earnings sustainably above 20.6p: a full year delivered at the top of the £60m to £65m range, or guidance raised again, with the growth rate demonstrably better than 2% into the following year.
  • A lower price: the arithmetic is a price problem as much as a profit one. At 191.17p the value opportunity is zero on these assumptions; below that the stance changes without the company having to do anything differently.

Bottom Line — Reduce

This is a good set of interim numbers from a company that in recent years seems to have been struggling to move forward. Revenue up +6.7% to £953.3m, gross margin up from 51.4% to 52.6%, underlying profit before tax up +66.0% to £39.0m, guidance raised to £60m–£65m, net debt down to £207.0m. Things do seem to be going in the right direction.

But around £8m of the first-half profit was warm weather on the company’s own estimate, the company itself says the first half’s like-for-like growth and gross margin will not continue, and I have a certain lack of confidence that we’re going to see significant uplifts in their profitability from this year’s type of figures into the medium term. That is why the growth factor is 2%.

Taking £62m of guidance, less £2.5m for supply chain optimisation, taxed at 25% over 216.58m shares gives 20.6p of earnings. At 2% growth and an 8× multiple that is 191.17p against a share price of 244.00p. The price already assumes the improvement carries on; I am not paying for weather.

Reduce. Value opportunity: -21.7%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: A.
