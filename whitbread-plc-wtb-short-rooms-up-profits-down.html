Rooms Up, Profits Down

Half-year results for the 26 weeks to 27 August 2026 (H1 FY27), with trading to 1 October 2026. Figures in sterling as the company reports them; Germany's revenue per available room is quoted in euros, again as reported.

Briefing

  • Thesis: They're struggling with their growth, and I think they are lost as to what to do next. The rooms keep being added, the restaurants are gone, and the profit still goes backwards — so I'm applying a 20% reduction to the valuation.
  • The numbers: Revenue up 2.0% to £1.57bn. Adjusted profit before tax down -9.1% to £286.8m. Statutory profit before tax down -62.0% to £109.1m after £177.7m of adjusting items. Adjusted earnings per share down -6.4% to 125.1p; statutory earnings per share 36.8p against 123.7p. Interim dividend held at 36.4p.
  • Key strength: UK and Ireland accommodation sales up 3.1% and up 4.5% in the five weeks to 1 October 2026, with revenue per available room growing 2 percentage points faster than the midscale and economy market, and 1,000 new rooms on course this year.
  • Key risk: The lease bill. Right-of-use depreciation and lease interest together took £25.7m out of profit, lease liabilities are £4.6bn, and lease-adjusted leverage is 3.4 times against the company's own 3.5 times ceiling.
  • Overall stance: Valuation 1,350.60p against a share price of 2,510p — a -46.2% value opportunity, 60% probability, research grade A. I consider Whitbread to be a short.
Note on the Short call: "Short" here is my stated view on the shares, not a disclosure that I hold a position in them. No market short-interest data is used anywhere in this analysis, and none should be inferred from the call.

Business Model

Whitbread is now a hotel business run as two divisions:

  • Premier Inn UK and Ireland — 842 hotels and 86,724 rooms, just over 90% of group revenue.
  • Premier Inn Germany — 75 hotels and 13,369 rooms, just under 10% of group revenue.

It sells budget hotel rooms, with food and beverage now sold to hotel guests only.

Accommodation was 79% of group revenue in the half against 77% a year ago. Food and beverage was 19% against 21%.

They are continuing their transition to more of a pure play hotel business, with all of their 197 branded restaurants closed in September 2026 and planning applications underway.

The open estate is 53% freehold and 47% leasehold by number of rooms in the UK and Ireland, and 24% freehold and 76% leasehold in Germany. The Five-Year Plan is to recycle £1.5bn of freehold property to fund the growth investment, so the freehold share will come down.

🎓 Clarity What do occupancy, average room rate and revenue per available room actually mean? Occupancy is the share of rooms filled. Average room rate (ARR) is the average price paid for the rooms that were filled. Revenue per available room (RevPAR) multiplies the two, so it measures what every room in the estate earns whether it is sold or not — it is the single number that tells you if a hotel group is really trading better. It matters here because Whitbread is shutting restaurants to lean harder on accommodation, so accommodation RevPAR has to do the work: in the UK and Ireland it grew 2.1% to £70.93, while in Germany it was flat at €62.

🎓 Clarity Why are there two profit figures, and which one is used in the valuation here? Statutory profit is the number under accounting rules — everything included. Adjusted profit strips out items management call one-off, here £177.7m of write-downs and restructuring costs. Both matter: the adjusted number shows how the hotels trade day to day, the statutory number shows what actually landed in the accounts. My valuation runs on an adjusted-basis earnings figure of 195.40p, not on the statutory 36.8p for the half or 123.3p for last year. That is the deliberate choice: the write-downs on restaurant and pipeline sites are mostly non-cash, so adjusted earnings are the fairer measure of the ongoing business — but it does mean the valuation ignores £135.9m of value destroyed on property this half.

What Is Pulling Down Profitability

Adjusted profit before tax fell -£29m, to £287m from £316m.

Revenue grew £31m and adjusted operating costs grew £22m to £963m, and earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation and rent (EBITDAR — a measure of trading profit before the cost of the buildings and the financing) rose £7m to £608m.

The company puts the cost growth down to inflation in the UK including business rates, transition costs on the Accelerating Growth Plan and the new openings in Germany, with £39m of cost savings going the other way.

The fall comes in the cost of the leases. Depreciation on right-of-use assets rose to £113.1m from £101.1m and interest on lease liabilities rose to £99.3m from £85.6m, a combined increase of £25.7m. The company puts this down to rent reviews and the sale and leaseback transactions completed last year.

Other depreciation and amortisation rose £6m to £112m, and net finance income outside the leases fell to £2m from £6m because there is less cash earning interest after the £250m buyback and the spending on the Accelerating Growth Plan.

Premier Inn Germany's £149.7m is so far away from the £1.4bn that the UK and Ireland are achieving.

There is just no way that, in the foreseeable future, Germany will be making a significant enough contribution to the overall group. They're just way too small.

Net Debt and the Long-Term Debt

Net debt has also risen to £663m from £563m the year before.

The two figures are not on the same basis. From this year BACS payments are counted when they settle and not when the payment instruction is created, under the amendments to IFRS 9, which adds to both cash and trade payables.

The company has not restated the comparatives. It says the change would have added £89.2m to cash at August 2025 and £69.2m at February 2026.

On the same basis net debt a year ago would have been around £474m, so the rise over the year is nearer £189m than £100m.

Against the February year end, the reported fall to £663m from £709m is the £69m accounting adjustment. The cash flow itself was an outflow of -£23m. Opening net debt plus the £23m cash outflow is £732m, and the £69m opening adjustment for how payments are counted brings it to £663m.

Adjusted operating cash flow was £389m after £192m of lease payments. Capital expenditure — capex, the money spent on building and refurbishing hotels — took £280m, tax £35m, interest £23m and the final dividend £101m, with £21m coming back from property disposals.

There was no sale and leaseback in the half, against £73m in the same half last year. The buyback is paused for this year.

The borrowings are three bonds totalling £950m, with an undrawn £775m revolving credit facility — a committed overdraft the company can draw on and repay — running to May 2029.

Cash was £275m against £831m a year ago, after the £450m bond that matured in October 2025 was repaid and the £250m buyback was completed.

The £300m bond due in May 2027 is the cheapest of the three. It has moved into current liabilities. On 22 September 2026 the company set up a £1.5bn Euro Medium Term Note programme — a standing shelf that lets it issue new bonds quickly — to support refinancing it.

That bond costs £7.1m a year in interest. The most recent bond, issued in February 2025, carries 5.50%, and at that rate the same £300m would cost £16.5m a year, £9.4m more.

Interest on the bonds was £17.9m in the half against £23.5m, with £10.8m of interest capitalised. Interest received on cash fell to £4.2m from £18.6m.

Lease liabilities are the much bigger number at £4.6bn, against £4.3bn a year ago. Net debt including lease liabilities was £5.3bn.

Interest on the leases was £99.3m in the half against £85.6m, and total net finance costs rose 22% to £97.1m from £79.3m.

Lease-adjusted net debt, the measure the company manages to in line with the Fitch method, rose to £3.6bn from £3.3bn, and lease-adjusted leverage rose to 3.4 times from 3.2 times.

The company's own ceiling is 3.5 times, which is also the Fitch threshold for a BBB- rating. Fitch rated the company BBB- with a stable outlook in May 2026, having confirmed it at BBB in February 2026.

On my arithmetic, £3,627m against £1,081.3m of earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation and rent over the last twelve months is 3.35 times, which leaves room for around £157m more lease-adjusted debt before the ceiling.

🎓 Clarity What is lease-adjusted leverage, and why does 3.5 times matter so much? Leverage compares debt with annual trading profit, so "3.4 times" means debt is 3.4 years of that profit. Lease-adjusted leverage treats rented hotels as if they were bought with borrowed money, because a 25-year lease is an obligation just like a loan — and Whitbread rents a lot: £4.6bn of lease liabilities against £950m of bonds. It matters because 3.5 times is both the company's own ceiling and the level Fitch uses for a BBB- credit rating, the lowest rung of investment grade. At 3.4 times there is only around £157m of headroom, so every sale and leaseback that lowers reported net debt pushes this number the other way.

The Restaurant Exit and the Property Sales

Property disposals brought in £21m in the half, £17m of it from the Accelerating Growth Plan sites.

The sites identified for disposal under the plan were written down by £29m in the half, and the pipeline sites being sold after the strategic review were written down by a further £62.1m to their sale value.

By October 2025 the company had sold 41 restaurant sites for £42m in total, around £1m a site.

I'm not necessarily against the company's move away from the food and beverage units. But I am not sure this is the best time for the company to be doing this, they're hardly selling the properties off at premium prices.

The Five-Year Plan and Growth

The Five-Year Plan set out in April aims, by FY31, for £275m of extra adjusted profit before tax from its growth initiatives against £110m of extra business rates, a 500 basis point rise in group return on capital employed and £2bn of free cash flow for shareholders.

Gross capital expenditure is cut by £1bn under the plan, and the growth investment is to be funded by recycling £1.5bn of freehold property. The company is targeting a return on capital employed of at least 15% on all growth capital expenditure.

The company's plan to recycle £1.5bn of freehold property to fund growth investment might make some sense if they were seeing a good uptick in revenues in their accommodation side of things, or if Germany was proving to be a growth market that was working out very well, or if they saw another market that pushing a huge amount of capital expenditure towards would make sense – but I do not see this as being the case.

Revenue was £2.96bn in the year to February 2024 and £2.92bn in each of the two years since. Adjusted profit before tax went from £561m to £483m and stayed there.

Adjusted basic earnings per share were 208.5p last year against 206.9p two years earlier, with the share count down to 167.14m from 184.12m. The dividend has been 97.0p in each of the three years.

I personally think they should be looking at other ways of increasing profitability. Maybe an acquisition of a whole operation in another country, or be working on other more outside-the-box items.

The hub by Premier Inn format has 19 hotels open in London and Edinburgh. Revenue per available room there grew 5% in the half, and the company says it has line of sight to more than 5,000 rooms by FY31.

To me, hubs in other countries, in a select few city centres, might be a potential way forward, because of their strong brand name.

I feel like they should at least be attempting these types of growth, as I do not think there is much evidence that their current plan is going to yield the results they want.

What Matters for This Company

  • Accommodation has to carry the whole thing: accommodation was 79% of revenue against 77%, and food and beverage is going to -37% in recent trading. UK and Ireland accommodation grew 3.1%, like-for-like 2%, revenue per available room 2.1%. That is what is left to grow the group.
  • The lease bill, not trading: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation and rent rose £7m, and right-of-use depreciation plus lease interest took £25.7m. The profit fall is a lease story, and the leases keep growing — £4,604.2m against £4,321.1m.
  • The 3.5 times ceiling: lease-adjusted leverage is 3.4 times, the company's own limit and Fitch's BBB- threshold, with around £157m of headroom on my arithmetic.
  • The property proceeds: £450m to £500m guided this year, £53m received, 173 sites agreed, and sites written down by £29m and £62.1m to get there. Around £1m a site on the 41 sold by October 2025.
  • Refinancing the May 2027 bond: £300m at 2.375% costs £7.1m a year; at the 5.50% of the February 2025 bond it would cost £16.5m, £9.4m more.
  • Germany's scale: £149.7m of revenue and a -£8m adjusted loss against £1.42bn and £313m in the UK and Ireland, with the pipeline cut to 4,823 rooms from 8,841.
  • Whether the plan is the right plan: £1.5bn of freehold recycled into growth capital expenditure, against three years of flat revenue and adjusted profit before tax down -13.9% from two years ago.

Valuation

They're struggling with their growth, so that's why I'm giving them a 20% reduction in the valuation.

I think they are lost as to what to do next, and I think that's going to continue to work quite negatively on the company's operations into the future.

As a result, I'm coming up with a valuation of 1,350.60p per share, which, given that the share price currently sits at 2,510p, gives it a -46.2% value opportunity.

I'm going to give it a high probability of 60%.

I think this company is therefore worth shorting. Given the recent history of the company failing to come up with a good plan for improving growth, and the environment in which they are currently operating, where margins are getting squeezed already, I think we are going to see further negative pressure on the company.

The arithmetic runs on 195.40p of earnings per share, a 1% growth rate and an 8 times multiple: ((195.40 × 0.01 × 8) + 195.40) × 8 = 1,688.26p, and the 20% reduction takes that to 1,350.60p. At 167.14m shares that is a valued market capitalisation of £2,257.4m against £4,195.2m today.

🎓 Clarity What does an earnings "override" mean in this model? Normally the model takes the last reported earnings per share. An override replaces that with the earnings figure I judge the business will actually deliver, and here it is 195.40p (override, FY2027e) rather than the 208.5p the company reported for FY26 on an adjusted basis. It matters twice over: it is the single biggest input into the 1,350.60p valuation, and because it is a forward number the price-to-earnings ratio shown in the snapshot (12.8 times) is the current 2,510p price measured against that estimate, not against a reported historic figure. Arithmetically, 195.40p sits about -6.3% below FY26's 208.5p — close to the -6.4% fall in adjusted earnings per share reported this half.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Sell case)

I should be straight about what would make this call wrong. The bull case is not empty:

  • Recent trading is accelerating: UK and Ireland accommodation sales were up 4.5% in the five weeks to 1 October 2026 and revenue per available room up 3.2% — faster than the 3.1% and 2.1% of the half.
  • London is working: London accommodation sales were up 7.2%, and hub by Premier Inn revenue per available room grew 5% with line of sight to more than 5,000 rooms by FY31.
  • Germany is turning on the recent run rate: accommodation sales up 31.2% in local currency in the five weeks to 1 October and revenue per available room up 9.5% to €83, with occupancy up 390 basis points in the half, the 17 established hotels at €71 against a market of €57, brand awareness at 22% from 17%, and the company still expecting a second consecutive year of profit.
  • Most of the damage is not cash: £135.9m of the £177.7m of adjusting items is impairment and write-offs, with only £7m of cash paid in the half.
  • Self-help is real: £39m of cost savings in the half held UK and Ireland operating costs flat at £834m, earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation and rent rose 1% to £588m, and return on capital employed rose to 13.0% from 11.8%.
  • The balance sheet is not stretched yet: £775m undrawn revolving credit facility, a £1.5bn Euro Medium Term Note programme in place since 22 September 2026, Fitch at BBB- stable, a £122m pension surplus, the dividend held, and £400m to £450m of property proceeds still to come this year.
  • The plan could deliver: £275m of extra adjusted profit before tax by FY31, £1bn less gross capital expenditure, a 500 basis point rise in return on capital employed and £2bn of free cash flow for shareholders. If the market believes it, the shares do not need the earnings first.
  • Shorting is asymmetric: a -46.2% value opportunity is my estimate of downside, not a cap on the loss if the shares go the other way, and the 3.90% dividend yield is a cost to a short position.

What Would Change My Mind

  • Accommodation actually accelerating, not just a five-week run: like-for-like accommodation growth holding well above the 2% of this half, with revenue per available room growth coming from the regions and not only London's 7.2%.
  • Lease costs stopping the bleed: adjusted profit before tax growing again despite right-of-use depreciation and lease interest, rather than being handed back by them as the £25.7m was here.
  • Property sold at decent prices: the £450m to £500m of proceeds delivered without further write-downs like the £29m and £62.1m taken this half — around £1m a site is not a premium price.
  • Germany reaching a size that matters: revenue and profit on a path towards the £65m of extra profit before tax targeted by FY31, from a -£8m loss and £149.7m of revenue today, with the pipeline rebuilt from 4,823 rooms.
  • A better plan: evidence they are attempting something beyond recycling freeholds into more of the same — an acquisition of a whole operation in another country, or hubs in a select few city centres abroad.
  • Leverage and refinancing: lease-adjusted leverage moving back down from 3.4 times rather than up through the 3.5 times ceiling, and the May 2027 £300m bond refinanced without adding the £9.4m a year that 5.50% would cost.

Bottom Line — Short

I believe that this is a sell, and that the company are going to struggle with profitability and growth options into the foreseeable future. I also think there will be some downward pressure on them in the coming year relating to the overall economic environment.

They're struggling with their growth, so that's why I'm giving them a 20% reduction in the valuation. I think they are lost as to what to do next, and I think that's going to continue to work quite negatively on the company's operations into the future.

As a result, I consider Whitbread to be a short!

Short. Value opportunity: -46.2%. Probability: 60%. Research grade: A.
