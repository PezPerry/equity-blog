Well Run, Priced for Growth That Isn't Coming

Briefing

  • Thesis: Wetherspoon sold more and earned less. Revenue rose 5.2% and like-for-like sales 4.2%, but profit before tax and separately disclosed items fell 28.0%. This is a well-run business with a property estate worth a good deal more than the balance sheet shows, but with earnings that will not escalate from here.
  • The numbers: Revenue £2,238.0m (+5.2%); operating profit before separately disclosed items £120.2m (-17.9%); operating margin 5.37% from 6.88%; profit before tax and separately disclosed items £58.6m (-28.0%); basic earnings per share 59.5p (60.0p); dividend held at 12.0p.
  • Key strength: Free cash flow nearly doubled to £100.1m, net debt excluding leases came down to £715.8m, and the freehold estate — not revalued since 1999 — may be worth around £875m more than the £336.8m of net assets on the balance sheet.
  • Key risk: Costs. £46m of wage increases, £31m of repairs and £9m of business rates. Consumer sentiment is not particularly strong, and government policy is not on their side on either alcohol sales or business rates.
  • Overall stance: Sell. My valuation is 647.36p against a 915p share price — a -29.3% value opportunity, on a 40% risk factor, 50% probability and a research grade of A+.

Business Model

Wetherspoon runs 792 managed pubs in the United Kingdom and the Republic of Ireland, plus 23 franchised pubs, selling drink, food, slot machines and hotel rooms at low prices on high volumes.

8 managed pubs opened in the year and there were 15 disposals, 10 trading pubs and five non-trading properties, which brought in £10.1m of cash. Fifteen franchised pubs opened, and the company plans around 15 managed pubs and 15 to 20 franchises in the current year.

Managed pub numbers moved from 794 to 792 over the year; the franchise estate went from 8 to 23 and the company plans 15 to 20 more in FY27. The company employs 24,200 full-time equivalents (FTEs — the headcount expressed as if everyone worked full-time hours), of which 19,095 are hourly-paid pub staff.

The freehold estate has been built up over the years by buying the freeholds of pubs where the company was the tenant: at the 2024 year end 72% of pubs were freehold, up from 41% in 2010. A freehold means the company owns the building and the land outright, rather than renting it under a lease.

FY26 Results: Sales Up, Profit Down

These are the full-year results for the 52 weeks to 26 July 2026. Revenue rose 5.2% to £2,238.0m, with like-for-like sales up 4.2%: bar sales up 6.1%, food up 1.2%, slot machines up 7.4% and hotel rooms up 1.3%.

Operating profit before separately disclosed items fell -17.9% to £120.2m, taking the operating margin down to 5.37% from 6.88%. The company puts the fall down to costs rising 5.3%, including £46m of wage increases, £31m of repairs and £9m of business rates.

Profit before tax (PBT — profit after all costs and interest but before the tax charge) and separately disclosed items fell -28.0% to £58.6m from £81.4m. After separately disclosed items, which were a £19.1m gain this year against a £7.9m gain last year, profit before tax was £77.7m against £89.3m.

Basic earnings per share (EPS — profit after tax divided by the number of shares) were 59.5p including the separately disclosed items, against 60.0p last year. Without them, earnings per share were only 42.4p against 50.8p.

Free cash flow — the cash left after running the estate, paying interest and tax and investing in the business — nearly doubled to £100.1m from £56.6m, helped by a much lower level of spending on existing pubs. Net debt excluding leases came down -1.2% to £715.8m. The dividend is held at 12.0p.

The company bought back 6,960,981 shares, 6.2% of the opening share count, for £46.0m at an average of 656p, and spent a further £28.5m on shares for the employee share scheme.

In the nine weeks to 27 September 2026 like-for-like sales rose 8.6%, which the company says was helped by exceptional weather, and it expects profit before tax and separately disclosed items for the current year in line with the company-compiled consensus of £74m.

Like-for-like (LFL) sales count only the pubs that traded in both years, so they strip out the effect of opening and closing sites. What Matters for This Company

Costs and the repairs line

Total employee costs increased 5.9% to £891.9m. Wages and salaries rose 4.0% to £787.0m while social security costs jumped 34.7% to £74.9m on the higher employer national insurance. I imagine this is generally not just an issue for the company. Higher wages and employer costs are a problem for most pubs/restaurants, even though they're a very efficient company, Wetherspoons are not immune to these rising costs.

Purchased food and drink, the inventory expense within cost of sales, was up 5.1% to £725.8m from £690.8m, which broadly matched sales growth. Employment charges ran roughly in line with the sales increase and food and drink basically ran with the increase in sales growth, which is maybe slightly disappointing.

Repairs and maintenance rose 32.3% to £132.0m from £99.8m, the biggest movement in the cost base. At the same time, reinvestment in existing pubs — capital expenditure, or capex, meaning money spent on the fabric and fittings of a pub and recorded on the balance sheet rather than charged straight to profit — fell to £26.3m from £62.5m. This may be related to the changing mix in their asset estate. The two lines together come to £158.3m, against £162.2m last year and £190.9m the year before.

The gross margin squeeze is possibly not real either: gross profit fell to £179.2m from £200.3m with cost of sales up 6.8% against revenue up 5.2%, and the repairs line sits within cost of sales. It is just changes in how the accounts are being reworked.

Finance costs and net debt

Net finance costs before separately disclosed items were £61.9m against £64.0m, with bank interest down to £41.1m from £45.1m as the cost of debt fell to 6.11% from 6.57%. After the separately disclosed finance credits of £20.8m, a £13.0m fair value gain on the interest-rate swaps and £7.8m of hedge reserve amortisation, net finance charges reduced -24.8% to £41.1m from £54.6m.

Net debt excluding leases reduced -1.2%, not very much but a little bit, to £715.8m from £724.3m. Including lease liabilities, debt fell to £1.11bn from £1.12bn. Cash sits at £52.8m, a nice increase from £38.7m.

I am pleased that they're reducing their net debt. Since the year end a £98m private placement — a loan placed directly with institutional lenders rather than raised on the public bond market — matured and was repaid from existing facilities rather than renewed, and a new £500m interest-rate swap has been taken out for February 2030 to February 2032 at 4.45%. An interest-rate swap is a contract that fixes the interest rate on floating-rate debt, so the company knows what its borrowing will cost.

Net debt excluding leases, from £724.3m to £715.8m. Operating cash of £284.3m was largely absorbed by interest and tax, capital spending, lease payments and £87.8m of buybacks, dividends and employee share purchases.

Cash flow and capital investment

Total capital investment was £74.2m against £117.0m. £28.7m of this was invested in new pubs and pub extensions, £26.3m in existing pubs, £5.6m in business and IT projects and £13.6m in freehold reversions of properties where Wetherspoon was the tenant — that is, buying out the freehold of a pub the company had been renting.

That's a reasonable level of reversion cost for the year, and it's down from £18.7m the previous year. Three reversions were completed. Still, £13.6m in freehold reversions in a year where there were 15 disposals seems a very high charge.

Free cash flow of £100.1m came after £31.9m of capital payments for existing pubs, business and IT projects (2025: £74.1m), £28.5m of share purchases for the employee share scheme and payments of tax and interest. Of that, £46.0m went on the buyback and £13.3m on dividends.

The property estate

The net book value of assets at the end of the period was £1.39bn, which the company says is 7.6 times the last twelve months' earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA — a rough proxy for trading cash profit) of £181.2m. The freeholds have not been revalued since 1999. That's 27 years since they revalued their freehold property, and the figure in the balance sheet seems misleading as a result.

🎓 Clarity What is "net book value", and how does it differ from net asset value? Net book value is what an asset cost, less the depreciation charged against it since — it is a history, not a price. Net asset value (NAV) is everything the company owns less everything it owes, and on Wetherspoon's balance sheet that comes to £336.8m. Because the freeholds have not been revalued since 1999, the book figure almost certainly understates what the pubs would fetch today. That is why the note argues there may be around £875m of hidden value above the stated net assets — and why the property argument is treated separately from the earnings argument below.

The separately disclosed items

I have to commend Wetherspoon, because they do like to rebase their accounts on what they see fit, one thing they do well is the income statement: they're providing greater clarity than they necessarily need to.

The chairman, Tim Martin, is very strident and has a lot of opinions. He does go on a bit about unfair tax treatment the company is subject to, which may be true, but I find it surprising that he's still going on about COVID and the treatment of the industry during lockdown. The tax system on pubs and bars is what it is, and I cannot see it changing anytime soon.

On the adjustments, property gains and losses were a loss of -£2.1m in this period, as opposed to a gain of £2.7m in the previous year. That's fair enough, and for future valuation I'd figure this at zero, because some years it'll be up and some years it'll be down.

🎓 Clarity Two earnings per share figures — 59.5p and 42.4p. Which one is this valuation built on? "Separately disclosed items" are gains and costs the company presents apart from trading profit because it regards them as one-offs: this year a £19.1m pre-tax gain, including a £13.0m fair value gain on interest-rate swaps and £7.8m of hedge reserve amortisation. Include them and basic EPS is 59.5p; exclude them and it is 42.4p. The valuation here uses the reported basic 59.5p, the figure that includes those items, so the snapshot below shows 59.5p and the price-to-earnings ratio is struck on it. On the 42.4p underlying figure the same formula would produce a lower valuation, so the choice made is the more generous of the two to the share price.

Where that leaves the business

I don't really share the market sentiment on this one at 915p. Despite being a great operator, I think they're going to struggle to see upward momentum in their earnings.

I've no doubt that greater implementation of AI and automated systems within their processes may improve results even more with time, but I do think they will struggle to improve EPS growth significantly.

Consumer sentiment is not particularly strong, and government policy is not on their side. It's not on their side as far as alcohol sales are concerned, and it's not on their side as far as business rates are concerned. It's also not looking like it's going to change any time soon.

My feelings are that this is a well-run business with a property estate worth a good deal more than the balance sheet shows, but with earnings that will not escalate from here.

Valuation

I'm using the basic earnings per share of 59.5p with a growth rate of 4.5%, which, if anything, could be seen as a little bit punchy. What is going to help the company on the upside in future years? Not a huge amount. What could affect it on the downside? Higher rates of inflation potentially increasing restriction in consumer spending, and potentially greater issues with government policy. As a result, I'm giving a risk factor of 40%.

My valuation comes in at 647.36p per share, which, given that the share price currently sits at 915p, gives it a -29.3% value opportunity.

No valuation uplift or reduction has been applied, and assets per share is not populated — so this is an earnings valuation, not an asset valuation, and the 4.5% growth rate and 8.0x multiple both do their work.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Sell case)

The bull case is a real one, and these are the things that would make the Sell wrong.

  • Current trading is running far ahead of my growth rate: like-for-like sales rose 8.6% in the nine weeks to 27 September 2026. The company says exceptional weather helped, but if anything close to that persists, 4.5% growth looks too low rather than punchy.
  • The repairs charge may not be a real cost increase: repairs and maintenance rose 32.3% to £132.0m while capital reinvestment in existing pubs fell to £26.3m from £62.5m. Combined spend of £158.3m is actually below £162.2m and £190.9m. If that reclassification unwinds, the margin squeeze unwinds with it.
  • The property estate is worth more than the accounts say: freeholds have not been revalued since 1999, and on the note's own assumptions there may be around £875m of value above the £336.8m of net assets. An earnings multiple does not capture that at all.
  • Cash generation is improving, not deteriorating: free cash flow nearly doubled to £100.1m, cash rose to £52.8m, and net debt excluding leases fell to £715.8m with the cost of debt down to 6.11% from 6.57% and a new £500m swap fixed at 4.45% for 2030–2032.
  • The share count is shrinking: 6,960,981 shares, 6.2% of the opening count, were bought back for £46.0m. Flat profit over a smaller share count still lifts earnings per share.
  • Franchising and automation: the franchise estate went from 8 to 23 with 15 to 20 more planned in FY27, and greater implementation of AI and automated systems within their processes may improve results even more with time.
  • A great operator can surprise: they are a very efficient company, and the market at 915p clearly takes a more constructive view of the earnings outlook than I do.

What Would Change My Mind

  • Earnings momentum beyond 4.5%: evidence that earnings per share can escalate from here, rather than the struggle to improve EPS growth significantly that I expect.
  • Like-for-like growth that survives the weather: the 8.6% in the first nine weeks holding up once the exceptional weather is out of the comparison, and FY27 profit before tax and separately disclosed items beating the £74m consensus.
  • A freehold revaluation: the company putting a current value on property it has not revalued since 1999, which would move the hidden asset value from my guesstimate into the accounts.
  • Margin recovery: the operating margin turning back up from 5.37% as the repairs line normalises, showing the cost increases really were a reclassification rather than a permanent step up.
  • A change in the policy backdrop: movement on alcohol sales or business rates — though it's also not looking like it's going to change any time soon.
  • Price: the shares moving towards my 647.36p valuation, which would close the -29.3% gap without anything changing in the business.

Bottom Line — Sell

I don't really share the market sentiment on this one at 915p. Despite being a great operator, I think they're going to struggle to see upward momentum in their earnings. This is a well-run business with a property estate worth a good deal more than the balance sheet shows, but with earnings that will not escalate from here.

On basic earnings per share of 59.5p, a growth rate of 4.5% that could be seen as a little bit punchy, and an 8.0x multiple, the valuation is 647.36p against a 915p share price. The research grade of A+ reflects the quality of the business and the clarity of the disclosure, not the attractiveness of the price.
