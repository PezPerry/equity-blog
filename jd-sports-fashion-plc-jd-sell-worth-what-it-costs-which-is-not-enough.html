Worth What It Costs, Which Is Not Enough

Second quarter trading statement for the thirteen weeks to 1 August 2026. JD Sports’ financial year ends in late January, so FY27 runs to 30 January 2027. All figures in sterling; per-share figures in pence.

Briefing

  • Thesis: Three bad years of profit, a lot of acquisitions, slipping profitability — and now the one thing they really cannot have, which is weak sales. This year is looking worse.
  • The numbers: Group like-for-like sales -3.1% in Q2 against -2.5% in Q1; North America -6.8%. Full-year profit before tax and adjusting items guided down to £700m–£800m from £750m–£850m, a midpoint -12.0% below the £852m just delivered.
  • Key strength: The shares are very lowly valued on a price/earnings ratio of around 8, free cash flow guidance is unchanged at £460m–£520m, and the second £100m tranche of the £200m buyback has begun.
  • Key risk: Spending is so weak in the United States market, which is where they were pinning a lot of growth hopes — and adjusting items have removed £1.09bn of pre-tax profit over four years, with a growing share of that the mechanical consequence of the acquisitions.
  • Overall stance: Valuation of 93.00p against a price of 92.42p is a value opportunity of +0.6%. That is no margin of safety at all. Sell, research grade C, probability 50%. Medium term, one to watch, potentially one to short.

Business Model

JD Sports Fashion is a global sports fashion retailer. It buys trainers and sportswear from the big brands and sells them on to consumers through its own stores and websites, across four reported regions: North America, Europe, the United Kingdom and Asia Pacific.

Two features of that model matter more than anything else here. The first is supplier concentration: Nike accounts for around 45% of JD’s sales, and Nike is still resetting its own business. The second is that a large part of recent growth has been bought rather than earned — the group has acquired heavily in recent years, with Hibbett and Courir both completing in FY2025.

Sales split roughly evenly between footwear and apparel by behaviour, if not by size: in this quarter footwear was soft while apparel performed well. Trade runs through stores, where footfall (the number of people walking through the door) was generally lower year on year outside key events, and online, which grew 2.6%.

🎓 Clarity What is the difference between like-for-like, organic and reported sales growth? Like-for-like (LFL) strips out new and closed stores and compares only shops and websites that have been trading in both periods — it is the cleanest read on whether existing customers are spending more. Organic adds back the effect of opening new space but still excludes businesses that were bought. Reported or headline growth includes everything, acquisitions included. The gap between the three is the whole argument here: reported revenue keeps rising, organic is barely positive, and like-for-like is falling. The till is doing less work than the headline suggests.

North America

On the North American market specifically, they are saying sales performance reflects weaker core consumer sentiment, and that back-to-school demand had been deferred from July into August.

They are indicating that it might not last. But this is not good. That spending is so weak in the United States market, which is where they were pinning a lot of growth hopes.

It is worth adding that the organic decline of -4.5% marks an acceleration from the first quarter, and North America is around 39% of group revenue on a full-year basis — larger than the 35% it represents in this particular quarter. This was the region the market had expected to stabilise first, which is why the shares fell around 13% on the day, wiping roughly £644m off the market value against a £50m reduction to the profit guidance midpoint.

What the charges were

  • FY2023 is the extreme year, with -£550.5m of adjusting items reducing an adjusted £991.4m to a statutory £440.9m. That period carried the costs of the strategic reset under the new management team, including significant store impairments, restructuring, and legal and regulatory charges relating to legacy competition matters.
  • FY2024 was the smallest of the four at -£106.2m, essentially acquisition-related costs and amortisation as the group worked through its purchases.
  • FY2025 charges rose again to around -£208m. This is the year the company changed its adjusting items policy to include the amortisation of acquired intangible assets, which is a direct consequence of the acquisition programme: Hibbett and Courir both completed in that year, and buying businesses creates intangible assets that must then be written down through the income statement every year afterwards.
  • FY2026 carried around -£223m, including £93m of impairment charges and £5m of associated costs on stores caught by the strategic review and closure programme, together with the continuing amortisation of acquired intangibles and the finance charges on the put options over minority interests in the acquired businesses.

The pattern is the important part. The charges are not one-off in any meaningful sense. They have run at over £100m in every one of the last four years and at over £200m in each of the last two, and a large and growing share of them is the mechanical consequence of acquisitions. They have done a lot of acquisitions in recent times and their profitability is slipping.

🎓 Clarity What are “adjusting items”, and why does amortisation of acquired intangibles keep coming back? Adjusting items are costs a company asks you to look past when judging underlying performance — restructuring, impairments (writing down the value of assets such as stores), legal charges. Amortisation is spreading the cost of an intangible asset over its useful life. When one company buys another, the price paid above the value of the physical assets is booked as intangible assets — brands, customer relationships — and those must then be written off through profit year after year. So an acquisition spree today guarantees a stream of “adjusting” charges for years afterwards. That is why a £1.09bn four-year total, running at over £200m in each of the last two years, should not be read as exceptional.

Earnings and Profitability

The company has seen the last two years of negative earnings per share growth. They have actually seen drops in their earnings per share figures as well as their total profitability.

On the company’s own adjusted basis, earnings per share have gone 13.4p, 12.8p, 12.4p and 11.6p across FY2023 to FY2026. That is three consecutive years of decline, and the estimate for the current year is the same!

Revenue has kept rising throughout, from £10.1bn to £12.7bn, but almost all of the recent growth was bought: 9.7% of the 10.5% revenue increase in FY26 came from the full-year effect of Hibbett and Courir, while organic growth was 2.1% and like-for-like was -2.1%.

What Matters for This Company

I am quite concerned for the company as to slipping sales in this market. They have had 3 bad years of profit, they have done a lot of acquisitions in recent times, their profitability is slipping, and this year is looking worse.

The one thing that they really cannot have now is weak sales. That is exactly what they have reported.

  • North America: around 39% of group revenue on a full-year basis, down -6.8% like-for-like and -4.5% organic, with the organic decline accelerating from the first quarter. This is where the growth hopes were pinned.
  • Nike concentration: around 45% of JD’s sales come from one supplier that is still resetting its own business.
  • The promotional market: the market remains highly promotional, which is what makes the flat-to-slightly-better 47.0% gross margin worth watching rather than celebrating.
  • The adjusting items gap: persistent, over £200m in each of the last two years, and increasingly a mechanical consequence of the acquisition programme rather than anything exceptional.
  • Income support: the yield is low at 1.3% at this rate, so there is little income support while you wait.
  • Governance: the chair is also changing, with Andrew Higginson having stepped down at the July annual meeting and an interim in place while a successor is found.
  • The share price record: the shares have lost around a quarter of their value over two years.

Valuation

Put plainly, using a middle ground of £750m pre-tax profit would equate to approximately £562.5m post-tax, or about 11.7p per share.

I am assuming a growth rate of 1%, and because of the concern about slipping sales I am applying a negative reduction on my valuation figures of -8%. I am also giving it a 40% risk factor, because I do not like where these figures are going and I think that there could be some issues to come.

Against that, they are very lowly valued: the company is sitting on a price/earnings ratio of 8 — 7.9x once recomputed against today’s 92.42p and the 11.70p earnings figure the valuation actually uses.

🎓 Clarity Why is the valuation built on 11.70p when the company’s last reported earnings per share was 8.5p? Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares. JD reports two versions: reported (statutory, after adjusting items) at 8.5p for FY2026, and adjusted (before them) at 11.6p. The 11.70p used here is neither — it is an override, FY2027e: my own forward figure, taken from the £750m midpoint of the freshly cut guidance range, taxed down to roughly £562.5m and divided across the share count. It sits on the adjusted basis, so it inherits the criticism made above: it flatters the statutory result by more than £200m a year. If you valued the shares on the 8.5p statutory figure instead, the arithmetic would be far less forgiving. That is a deliberate choice to give the company the benefit of its own measure and still find no upside.

This gives a valuation of 93.00p against a current share price of 92.42p — a value opportunity of +0.6%. However, I think this is still a sell. I definitely would not be buying right now. I would be concerned about further negative news coming through about this company.

The reason the recommendation runs against the sign of the value opportunity is that +0.6% is no margin of safety at all. It says the shares are worth roughly what they cost, on an earnings figure that is itself the midpoint of a range the company has just cut, in a market where the last two quarters have each been worse than the one before. If the second half lands at the bottom of the range rather than the middle, the valuation falls below the price.

🎓 Clarity What is a “margin of safety”, and why does +0.6% not count as one? A margin of safety is the gap between what you think something is worth and what you have to pay for it. It exists because valuations are estimates, and estimates are wrong. A 30% gap can absorb a bad quarter; a 0.6% gap absorbs nothing. Here the estimate rests on the midpoint of a guidance range that was cut this morning — move to the bottom of that range and the valuation drops straight through the share price. The risk factor of 40% and probability of 50% say the same thing in different units: this is a coin-flip resting on a number that has been moving the wrong way.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong (for the Sell case)

The bull case is real and should be stated plainly, because at this price it does not take much to make the sell wrong.

  • The deferral is genuine: the company says back-to-school demand was deferred from July into August. If that is right, the third quarter picks up what the second lost and North America looks less broken than -6.8% suggests.
  • Cash is holding: free cash flow guidance of £460m–£520m is unchanged against £462m delivered in FY2026. Only the profit line moved.
  • They are buying their own shares: the second £100m tranche of the £200m buyback has begun, at a price that is a quarter below where it was two years ago.
  • Parts of the group are working: the United Kingdom at +0.8% and Asia Pacific at +1.4% were positive like-for-like, Asia Pacific organic growth was +10.2%, online grew 2.6% and apparel performed well across all regions.
  • The rating is genuinely low: a price/earnings ratio of around 8 does not demand much to go right, and if the second half lands at the top of the guidance range rather than the middle, the earnings figure the valuation is built on rises and the valuation rises with it.
  • Nike: the supplier that is around 45% of sales is resetting its business. A reset that completes is a tailwind as surely as a reset that drags is a headwind.

What Would Change My Mind

  • North America stabilising: organic growth in the region turning back towards flat rather than accelerating downwards from -4.5%.
  • The deferred demand actually arriving: a third quarter that reverses the August deferral instead of extending the trend, after two quarters each worse than the one before.
  • Guidance held or raised: the £700m–£800m range confirmed at the interim stage, with the outturn tracking the upper half rather than the lower.
  • Adjusting items falling back: a year where the charges drop meaningfully below the £200m-plus of the last two, and where what remains is not simply the amortisation of what they bought.
  • The earnings decline breaking: adjusted earnings per share rising rather than making it four consecutive years of decline from 13.4p.
  • A permanent chair: a successor appointed to replace Andrew Higginson, removing the interim arrangement at the top of the board.

Bottom Line — Sell

Three bad years of profit, a lot of acquisitions, profitability slipping, and this year looking worse. The one thing they really cannot have now is weak sales, and that is exactly what they have reported.

My model gives 93.00p against 92.42p. That is a value opportunity of +0.6%, which is no margin of safety at all — and it sits on the midpoint of a range the company cut this morning. I definitely would not be buying right now, and I would be concerned about further negative news coming through about this company.

Medium term, one to watch, potentially one to short.
