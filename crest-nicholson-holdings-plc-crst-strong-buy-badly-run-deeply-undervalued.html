Business Model

Crest Nicholson is a housebuilder. It sells homes on the open market, sells bulk and affordable units to housing associations and investors, and sells land from its larger sites.

Turnover has fallen from £914m in 2022 to £611m in 2025, and pre-tax profit (PBT — profit before tax is deducted) from £131m to £8m.

Completions have gone from 1,873 in 2024 to 1,691 in 2025 and 1,353 in 2026, down −20.0% on the year.

The Year to 31 October 2026

Crest Nicholson have come up with a trading and balance sheet update for the year ending 31 October 2026.

The company ended the year with net debt of approximately £64m. This was better than the previous guidance range of £70m to £90m. In the company's words, cash generation, working capital and liquidity management "remain the Group's immediate priorities" while housing market conditions remain challenging.

Full year completions were 1,353, at the bottom of the previous guidance range of 1,350 to 1,400.

The company is again highlighting weak demand across the sector, driven by an uncertain geopolitical and macroeconomic environment. The open market sales rate fell to 0.34 across September and October, against 0.48 in the first half.

The group now expects current challenging trading conditions to persist well into next year. It is reviewing the profitability of sites in the short-term land bank, with net realisable value provisions of £10m to £15m taken against the year.

Adjusted earnings before interest and tax for the year are expected to be a loss in the range of −£30m to −£40m, against a loss of around −£10m guided in September.

🎓 Clarity What is a "net realisable value provision", and why does a housebuilder take one? A housebuilder carries its land and part-built homes on the balance sheet as inventory, at what it cost. Net realisable value is what it now expects to actually get for them, after costs to finish and sell. If that is lower than cost, accounting rules require it to write the difference off as a provision — a charge today for value that has already gone. Crest's £10m to £15m of provisions against the year tells you it has looked at sites in its short-term land bank and concluded some of them will not make the money it once assumed. That matters here because the whole case rests on what the assets are really worth, which is exactly why I take the stated net asset value and then cut it further myself.

Profit guidance has been cut at every update this year. The year started with an expected adjusted pre-tax profit of £32m to £40m and has finished with an adjusted operating loss of −£30m to −£40m.

Net debt has come in better than the last two sets of guidance, though at around £64m it is still above the £38.2m at the end of 2025.

Exceptional items

  • The company is expected to require an additional provision of £15m to £20m for fire remediation.
  • It also announced restructuring costs of around £10m, relating to the closure of a divisional office and the costs of the ongoing covenant waiver. These will be treated as an exceptional charge.
  • Recoveries on fire remediation in the year are expected to exceed the cost increases.
  • The net exceptional charge for 2026 is expected to be £20m to £25m.

Lenders

The company says it is still in constructive discussions with its lenders to amend its covenants. The debt facilities are under an interest cover covenant waiver, last extended to 30 November 2026, and the company is also looking at alternative debt financing.

I am concerned at how long it is taking them to get through this discussion with lenders.

🎓 Clarity What is an "interest cover covenant waiver", and why does it matter so much? A covenant is a promise in a loan agreement. An interest cover covenant says profits must stay at least a set multiple of the interest bill. With the company now guiding to a loss, that test cannot be met — so the lenders have agreed to set it aside for a period, which is the waiver, last extended to 30 November 2026. A waiver is temporary permission, not a fix: if it lapses without being replaced by amended covenants or new financing, lenders can in principle demand their money back. That is the single biggest reason I discount the assets by 30% rather than taking the balance sheet at face value.

Outlook for 2027

For the full year 2027 the company says it expects to continue to trade with adjusted operating losses while it focuses on cash generation and liquidity management. Full guidance will come with the preliminary results in January 2027.

The company is selling things cheap in order to generate cash to keep the operations going.

My read

I don't doubt that the macroeconomic situation is impacting the company. However, other housebuilders are not seeing these same issues.

I think Crest Nicholson are not very well managed, and that is the source of a lot of their current problems. The company has a short to medium term cash issue, and it is badly run.

That doesn't take away from the fact that there is significant value here to be pulled out. What it has in its system is still a land bank of real value, and there are options available. This isn't an unsellable list of assets.

My feelings are that, with the share price now equating to a market capitalisation of £113.4m, Crest Nicholson is a very good option for a bigger construction company to purchase and insert into its existing operations.

Galliford Try in particular is trying to revamp its property side. Crest Nicholson would give it a large amount of additional property to insert into its relatively new operations, at a great discount.

Valuation

Things may have deteriorated, so I'm taking the net asset value at 30 April 2026 of £688.8m and reducing it by a further £80m, giving a net asset value of £608.8m.

I still think that primarily the assets are relevant. Inventory stood at £1,052.5m at that point.

While not the most liquid of assets, inventory is still semi-liquid. Available land and property can be sold off.

I am discounting the net asset value by 30% for the issues with the covenants, execution risk and the likelihood that the company has to discount things in order to sell them. That still gives a net asset value of £426.2m, or 165.98p per share.

Against the current market capitalisation of £113.4m, there's significant value sitting there. I don't doubt the company has a lot of work to do but they also have a lot of value.

On the current share price of 44.15p, this gives a value opportunity of +275.9%, a strong buy.

🎓 Clarity What is net asset value, and why value this one on assets instead of earnings? Net asset value (NAV) is everything the company owns minus everything it owes — here, mostly land and part-built homes, less debt. Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by the number of shares, and the price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) is the share price divided by EPS. Those earnings measures are close to useless when a company is loss-making: a tiny or negative profit makes the P/E meaningless. So this valuation runs in asset mode — the figure that drives it is asset value per share, and the growth rate and valuation multiple in my model are ignored entirely. EPS is shown in the snapshot below for completeness, but it plays no part in the number, which is also why the reported-versus-adjusted earnings debate doesn't change the answer here.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong

  • The lender discussions: The facilities sit under an interest cover covenant waiver last extended only to 30 November 2026, and the company is still in discussions to amend its covenants. I am concerned at how long it is taking them to get through this discussion with lenders.
  • Losses continue into 2027: The company expects to continue to trade with adjusted operating losses through the full year 2027, with full guidance not arriving until the January 2027 preliminary results.
  • Guidance keeps moving the wrong way: Profit guidance has been cut at every update this year — from an adjusted pre-tax profit of £32m to £40m in January to an adjusted operating loss of £30m to £40m in November.
  • Demand is still falling: The open market sales rate dropped to 0.34 across September and October from 0.48 in the first half, and the group expects challenging conditions to persist well into next year.
  • More value could come out of the land bank: The company is reviewing the profitability of sites in the short-term land bank, with £10m to £15m of net realisable value provisions already taken. It is selling things cheap in order to generate cash to keep the operations going, which is why I reduced the net asset value by a further £80m and then discounted it 30%.
  • Management: I think Crest Nicholson are not very well managed, and that is the source of a lot of their current problems.
  • Net debt is still rising: £64m at the year end against £38.2m at the end of 2025, even after beating the £70m to £90m guidance.

What Would Change My Mind

  • The covenant talks fail: If the waiver is not replaced by amended covenants or alternative debt financing, the short to medium term cash issue stops being a timing problem.
  • The land bank review keeps taking value out: Net realisable value provisions materially beyond the £10m to £15m already taken would eat into the asset base the whole case rests on.
  • January guidance for 2027 is worse again: Guidance has been cut at every update this year; another cut at the preliminary results would say the cash burn is deeper than the company itself expects.
  • The assets turn out to be harder to sell than I think: My case is that inventory is semi-liquid and this isn't an unsellable list of assets. Evidence that land and property cannot be moved except at far worse discounts than 30% would change the valuation.
  • The takeover route closes: A large part of the appeal is that at a £113.4m market capitalisation this is a very good option for a bigger construction company to buy and insert into its existing operations.

Bottom Line — Strong Buy

The company has a short to medium term cash issue, and it is badly run. I don't doubt the macroeconomic situation is hurting it, but other housebuilders are not seeing these same issues, and I think management is the source of a lot of the current problems.

None of that takes away from the fact that there is significant value here to be pulled out. Inventory of £1,052.5m, a land bank of real value, and options available — this isn't an unsellable list of assets. Take the April net asset value of £688.8m, cut £80m off it for further deterioration, discount the remaining £608.8m by 30% for the covenants, execution risk and the discounts needed to sell, and you still have £426.2m, or 165.98p per share, against 44.15p in the market.

At a £113.4m market capitalisation, Crest Nicholson is a very good option for a bigger construction company to purchase and insert into its existing operations — Galliford Try in particular, which is trying to revamp its property side and would get a large amount of additional property at a great discount.

Strong Buy. Value opportunity: +275.9%. Probability: 50%. Research grade: A+.
