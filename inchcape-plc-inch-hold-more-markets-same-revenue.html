  • Thesis: Revenue was £9,380m in 2019 and £9,100m in 2025 — the group is no bigger today than it was before COVID. The gross margin has done all the work, rising from under 14% to 17%. Business Model

Inchcape sits between vehicle manufacturers and markets. A manufacturer will want to sell in, say, Chile, Ethiopia or Bulgaria, but doesn't want to build a national infrastructure and organisation, so instead it appoints a distributor.

That distributor imports the vehicles, holds the inventory, prepares them and supports a dealer network. It runs the brand's marketing, stocks and distributes parts, and manages the warranty administration.

Inchcape very much sits in the small and medium-sized markets that don't justify a full subsidiary from the manufacturer. In some markets it also owns the dealerships itself, and in Poland it is a BMW retailer without holding the distribution contract.

🎓 Clarity What is the difference between a distributor and a dealer? A dealer runs a showroom and sells cars to the public in one town. A distributor holds the national contract for a brand in a whole country: it buys the vehicles from the factory, finances and warehouses them, markets the brand, supplies the dealers, stocks the spare parts and handles warranty claims. Inchcape is mostly the distributor, which is why it carries so much inventory and so much vehicle funding on its balance sheet — and why its margin is thin on a very large revenue number.

FY2025 Results — The Year in Numbers

These are the full-year results for the year to 31 December 2025. Revenue fell 1.8% to £9,100m from £9,263m, flat at constant currency and up 1% organically.

The gross margin slipped to 17.0% from 17.3%, with gross profit of £1,550m against £1,606m.

Adjusted operating profit fell 4% to £563m from £584m, a margin of 6.2% against 6.3%. The Americas and Europe and Africa both grew profit; the Asia Pacific division fell 23%.

Adjusted profit before tax was £443m, down £1m on £444m, with net finance costs reduced to £123m from £150m. Reported profit before tax was £406m against £414m after £37m of adjusting items.

Adjusted basic earnings per share rose 13% to 80.8p from 71.3p, helped by the buyback of 33.5m shares, 9% of the share capital, for £234m. Basic earnings per share from continuing operations were 72.5p against 66.4p.

Free cash flow was £315m against £462m, a conversion of 104% of adjusted profit after tax. Adjusted net debt, excluding leases, rose to £264m from £190m, leverage of 0.4 times.

The dividend is up 13% to 32.3p, 40% of adjusted earnings per share.

That gap matters here because the group earns in roughly 40 currencies, and in 2025 currency took most of the Americas' 8% organic growth back out again.

The gross margin has risen from under 14% before COVID to 17% to 18% since 2023 while revenue has gone nowhere. The 2018 and 2019 bars include the UK retail business; from 2023 the figures are the restated continuing business, and 2023 as originally reported was £11,447m.

Accelerate+ and the Contract Pipeline

The growth plan, Accelerate+, was launched in November 2024. Its core pillars are scale and optimisation.

Scale means growing market share, partly via acquisitions, partly through new distribution contracts and partly through expansion into adjacent vehicle categories such as premium motorcycles and light commercial vehicles. The group has around 3% share of the vehicles sold in its markets and wants to drive that towards 10% over the medium to long term.

Optimisation is about ensuring the business is more efficient, and about leveraging value-added services like parts, finance and insurance products. The medium-term targets set in March 2025 are organic volume growth of 3% to 5% a year, operating margins of around 6%, earnings per share growth of more than 10% a year, £2.5bn of free cash flow between 2025 and 2030, a 40% dividend payout and leverage below one times.

That is all good, and it sounds pretty straightforward from that point of view. What I would generally say is that ensuring your business operates as efficiently as it can isn't, in my opinion, a plan. That's something you should be doing anyway. A growth plan sets out specific targets and the ways in which you're going to grow the brand or the business's profitability, in ways that are maybe slightly different to the everyday.

Since 2022 the company has won more than 50 new distribution contracts, 52% of them in the Americas, 30% in Europe and Africa and 18% in Asia Pacific, so there has been growth in all of the regions, which is positive.

The rate of wins is decreasing, though. There were 15 in 2023, a record 22 in 2024, 10 in 2025 and five in 2026 so far.

Contract wins peaked at 22 in 2024 and have fallen since, while the exits of small, dilutive contracts went from four in 2024 to 15 in the first half of 2026 alone.

🎓 Clarity What are "adjusting items", and why does PBT come in two sizes? PBT is profit before tax — what the business earned before the taxman takes a share. Reported PBT is the statutory number under the accounting rules. Adjusted PBT is management's number, with certain costs stripped out as one-offs: here £37m of acquisition, disposal and restructuring charges. That is why FY2025 shows adjusted PBT of £443m and reported PBT of £406m for the same year. The judgement call is whether the "one-offs" keep recurring — Inchcape's operating adjusting items were £100m in 2021, £11m in 2022, £50m in 2023, £22m in 2024 and £37m in 2025, and another £64m landed in the first half of 2026.

The £10m of acquisition and integration costs is the tail of the Derco integration in South America, a multi-year programme now complete with cumulative costs of £70m. The £4m disposal loss is a completion-accounts adjustment in the buyer's favour on the Chilean parts business sold in December 2024 for £30m. The £23m of restructuring is cost reduction started in 2025 plus group-wide back-office transformation, a review of organisational structures, processes and the physical location of certain operations, continuing into the first half of 2026.

The 2024 items were £42m of Derco integration, a £6m gain on the Chilean parts sale, a £19m reversal of a previous impairment of the Suzuki distribution agreement in Central America, a £5m write-off of a Bolivian distribution agreement and an £8m hyperinflation charge from Ethiopia, which stopped being treated as hyperinflationary from 1 January 2025.

There is no amortisation of acquired intangibles in the adjustments, because the distribution agreements and brands are held as indefinite-life assets. Operating adjusting items over five years were £100m in 2021, £11m in 2022, £50m in 2023, £22m in 2024 and £37m in 2025, and the cash paid against them in 2025 was £28m.

In the first half of 2026 adjusting items were £64m, of which £62m was restructuring, mostly in Asia Pacific, against £14m in the first half of 2025.

The Cash Flow Hedge Losses

The net fair value losses on cash flow hedges of £83m sit in the statement of comprehensive income, below profit for the year, not in the income statement. Last year the same line was a gain of £22m.

The hedges are forward foreign exchange contracts. Most of the group's businesses buy vehicles from the manufacturers in their own currency, but Australia buys in Japanese yen and the South and Central American businesses buy in yen and US dollars. The group sells its local currency forward to lock in the cost of vehicles it has committed to buy, and at the year end it had £1,862m of these contracts outstanding, maturing by December 2026, at a weighted average rate of 99 yen to the Australian dollar and 940 Chilean pesos to the US dollar.

Because they hedge future purchases, the contracts are marked to market through the hedging reserve rather than through profit. When the vehicles arrive the accumulated gain or loss on the matching contracts is added to the cost of that inventory, £29m of losses in 2025, and it then reaches the income statement through cost of sales as the cars are sold. Nothing goes straight to profit; the ineffective portion of the hedges was nil.

The trade payables are quite high, and I would have some slight reservations. The bulk of the figure is not trade creditors though, which actually fell to £243m from £260m and are paid in 15 to 40 days. It's the £1,762m of vehicle funding, up £180m in the year.

Vehicle funding is stocking finance. The group pays for its new vehicles, and part of its used stock, with facilities from various lenders including the manufacturers' own finance companies, and repays each loan when the vehicle is sold or at maturity, whichever comes first. The facilities are generally uncommitted, run for 180 days or less and in some cases give the lender security over the vehicles. £1,304m of the £1,762m is from non-manufacturer lenders where the manufacturer has already been paid, so it's bank finance in all but name. The interest on it was £54m in 2025, at a weighted average rate of 3.5% against 5.1%, and it sits in finance costs.

The company classifies vehicle funding as a trade payable rather than as debt, so the reported net debt of £607m, and the leverage of 0.4 times, exclude it. The payment terms are 70 to 100 days.

🎓 Clarity If £1,762m of vehicle funding is borrowed money, why isn't it in the debt figure? Leverage is net debt divided by profits — a measure of how much a company owes relative to what it earns. Inchcape reports net debt of £607m and leverage of 0.4 times, which looks very comfortable. But that excludes £1,762m of vehicle funding, which the company classifies as a trade payable because the borrowing is tied to specific cars sitting in its own inventory. Include it and the picture looks heavier. The honest read is the one in the note: the vehicles themselves are the security, this is how the industry funds its stock, and the group had around £880m of its £900m revolving credit facility undrawn. The thing to watch is that these facilities are uncommitted and short-dated.

The risk, if times get a bit tough, is that these are uncommitted and short-dated facilities, so lenders could tighten them at the point when stock was slowest to sell. Against that, the vehicles themselves are the security, stocking finance is how the industry funds its vehicles, and the group had £657m of cash at the year end, £261m net of £396m of overdrafts in its cash pooling arrangements, with around £880m of the £900m revolving credit facility undrawn.

What Matters for This Company

Three things carry the case, and all three point the same way.

The top line has stopped. Revenue was £9,380m in 2019 and £9,100m in 2025. Everything that has gone right in six years has gone right in the margin — under 14% gross then, 17.0% now — and the margin has been edging back down for two years in a row.

The divisional swing factor is Asia Pacific. It was 28% of 2025 revenue, its profit fell 23% in FY2025, and in the first half of 2026 it halved to £39m with the margin down from 6.4% to 3.5%. The other two divisions are doing their job; this one has taken the group's first-half profit to flat.

The growth plan is an acquisition plan. Askja and Silver Star cost £209m for around £390m of annual revenue, and more is coming. That is capital out now for earnings later.

I think they are a little bit at the whim of what is happening in global supply chains, and even US foreign policy, which may not really help them given the current inclination towards brands controlling their own supply chains and the complexities of trying to manage international trade. I think that does have a bit of a negative impact on the company, albeit they are not primarily dealing with the United States.

I don't really love their growth plan. Purchasing up acquisitions of distributors and dealerships is not the worst plan ever, but it is a costly one, and I think earnings per share growth will trail the capital expenditure on widening their distribution.

🎓 Clarity Why does the valuation use 80.8p when reported earnings were 72.5p? EPS is earnings per share — the profit attributable to each individual share. Inchcape publishes two: adjusted basic EPS of 80.8p, which excludes the £37m of acquisition, disposal and restructuring charges, and reported basic EPS from continuing operations of 72.5p, which includes them. The model uses the adjusted 80.8p, entered manually as an override rather than pulled from a data feed, so every ratio on this page follows that same basis: the price-to-earnings ratio is 685.50p ÷ 80.8p = 8.5x. On the reported 72.5p the same price would be 9.5x — a roughly one-turn difference, worth knowing before you compare this to anything else.
🎓 Clarity What does the model actually do with growth rate, multiple and risk factor? The valuation is mechanical: take EPS, add one year of growth valued at the multiple, then capitalise the lot at the same multiple. Here that is ((80.8p × 3% × 8) + 80.8p) × 8 = 801.54p. No uplift or reduction was applied, so the formula valuation and the actual valuation are the same number. The risk factor of 36% and the probability of 40% do not feed the arithmetic — they are separate judgements about how much could go wrong and how likely the thesis is to play out, and here they are what turn a positive value opportunity into a Hold rather than a Buy.

Risks and What Could Go Wrong

  • Asia Pacific: 28% of 2025 revenue, profit down 23% in FY2025 and halved to £39m in the first half of 2026 with the margin down from 6.4% to 3.5%. Australia was the problem — fuel price disruption pushing buyers to cheaper and new energy vehicles, a key brand partner with supply constraints, and squeezed gross margins.
  • Supply chains and politics: they are a little bit at the whim of global supply chains and even US foreign policy, with the current inclination towards brands controlling their own supply chains and the complexities of international trade. That does have a bit of a negative impact, albeit they are not primarily dealing with the United States.
  • The growth plan costs before it pays: buying up distributors and dealerships is not the worst plan, but it is a costly one, and earnings per share growth is likely to trail the capital expenditure on widening their distribution.
  • The win rate is falling: 15 contracts won in 2023, a record 22 in 2024, 10 in 2025 and five in 2026 so far, while exits went from four in 2024 to 15 in the first half of 2026 alone. BYD will not renew in Belgium and Luxembourg when the contract expires in the third quarter of 2027, and Changan ended its Philippines contract in August.
  • Vehicle funding: £1,762m of it, up £180m in the year, excluded from the £607m of reported net debt. The facilities are uncommitted and short-dated, so lenders could tighten them at exactly the point when stock was slowest to sell.
  • Hedged 2026 margins: the £46m after-tax hedge reserve unwinds through cost of sales during 2026, so next year's gross margin reflects locked-in rates that are worse than spot. An opportunity cost rather than a cash loss, but it lands in the margin.
  • Thin margins and a flat top line: a 17.0% gross margin and a 6.2% operating margin leave little room, and the gross margin has now slipped two years running (17.7%, 17.3%, 17.0%) on revenue that has gone nowhere since 2019.
  • Cash going out of the door: £339m returned to shareholders in 2025 against £315m of free cash flow, which is what pushed adjusted net debt up £74m. It's the buybacks, not the acquisitions, absorbing the cash.

What Would Change My Mind

  • A wider gap to 801.54p: the valuation stands; the issue is that +16.9% isn't a high enough value opportunity to purchase them. A materially wider discount to 801.54p would change the action without changing a single assumption.
  • Asia Pacific actually stabilising: the company says Asia has stabilised and it exited 13 contracts there in the half. Evidence of that in the second-half numbers — margin recovering from 3.5% — would support a higher growth rate than the 3% I've used.
  • Earnings per share growth arriving ahead of the spend: my view is that earnings per share growth will trail the capital expenditure on widening distribution. Delivery of the company's more than 10% earnings per share growth target, with the acquisitions converting, would undercut that.
  • The win rate turning back up: five contracts in 2026 to date against 22 in 2024. A return towards that rate would say the pipeline, rather than acquisitions, is doing the growing.
  • The political and supply chain picture easing: the 36% risk factor reflects the international political stage and things going wrong in the meantime. If that pressure eased, the risk factor comes down.

Bottom Line — Hold

This is a business that has made and is making forward movement, but its earnings are at the mercy of supply chains and politics it doesn't control, and its growth plan will cost money before it earns it. The margin has done all the heavy lifting since 2019 while the top line has gone nowhere, and the division that is supposed to be a third of the group has halved its first-half profit.

On adjusted earnings per share of 80.8p, a 3% growth rate and an 8x multiple, I get 801.54p against a price of 685.50p. That is a positive gap, and the balance sheet, the 28.8% return on capital employed and the 4.70% dividend yield all support it — but I don't think it's a high enough value opportunity for this company to purchase them.

Hold. Value opportunity: +16.9%. Probability: 40%. Research grade: A.
